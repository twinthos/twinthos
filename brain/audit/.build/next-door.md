The last list was good research and a hard sell. Every firm on it has admin. Every one has also been pitched AI three times this year, so the call starts in a crowded room.

This book starts somewhere else.

It looks for firms that get paid **per completed piece of work** — per claim, per case, per renewal, per placement, per funding claim. In those firms, admin capacity is the ceiling on revenue. Slow admin means money that never arrives.

That changes what you are to the person on the phone. You are not the consultant asking them to shrink their own team. You are more capacity in a business that is paid by the unit. That is a promotion, not a threat.

Three things follow from it:

- The first step gets small. One job, two weeks, a fixed fee. One person can say yes.
- The pitch gets short. "You bill per claim. Your team can process this many a week. This makes it that many."
- The monthly number arrives after a result, not before one.

## Why the last book stalls {#why-it-stalls}

Three reasons. Each one has a fix, and the fix is why this list is different.

### 1. You ask them to shrink their own team

The person who owns the admin queue is the person whose team gets smaller. Nobody gets promoted for that. They say "interesting" and nothing happens.

**Fix:** pick firms where more throughput means more revenue. Then the queue getting faster is their win, and they will fight for it internally.

### 2. The price needs a second signature

At £60,000 a year, an owner-managed firm has to discuss it. A discussion is a delay, and delays die.

**Fix:** make the first step one person's decision. One job, two weeks, £3,500. The monthly fee is a separate conversation held after the thing has already worked.

### 3. Nothing has a date on it

"Would you like this?" has no deadline. A diary with no date in it is a wish.

**Fix:** only call firms that already have a date. A renewal, a funding return, an audit, a claim window, a go-live.

## The five-minute test {#five-minute-test}

Answer all five before you dial. If you cannot, do not dial.

| # | Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What do they get paid for? | Per-unit firms have a reason to say yes |
| 2 | How many units a month? | This is the size of the prize, in their language |
| 3 | Who signs without asking anyone? | The name on the call sheet |
| 4 | What date forces it? | The reason the call happens this month |
| 5 | Can it run without patient data? | Keeps you out of the regulated queue |

A firm that passes all five is worth an hour. A firm that passes three is worth an email. A firm that passes two is a bookmark.

## The price {#price}

Four numbers exist in the current material and they do not agree: £750 a month, a £1,500 pilot, £5,000 a month, £60,000 a year. `company/pricing.md` contains none of the £1,500. A buyer cannot act on a document that contradicts itself, and neither can you.

Here is what the evidence outside your own files says.

| Source | What it charges | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Nick Vasilescu, first client, 2025 | $3,500 for one agent, sold in week one | One job, one install, paid up front |
| Belgian web designer, Aug 2026 | €2,750 install, €267 a month | Small monthly is enough if the install is paid |

| UK managed-agent band, Oct 2026 | £750–£2,000 a month per role | £5,000 for one role is outside the band |

The pattern across all four: **the install is paid, the monthly is modest, and the job is narrow.** Your £5,000 monthly for one role sits above every UK comparable. Your £1,500 pilot sits below what operators charge for an install.

A ladder that fits both the evidence and your own test prices:

| Step | Price | What it is | Who can sign it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The install | £3,500 | One job, two weeks, measured against today's numbers | One person |
| One job, running | £1,250 a month | The same job, maintained, with a monthly evidence page | One person |
| Three jobs | £2,950 a month | Three defined jobs on one brain | One person, still under most limits |
| The audit | £999 | Two days, a written map of where the hours go | Anyone |

The arithmetic you can say out loud: one admin on £28,000 costs about £35,000 loaded. One job at £1,250 a month is £15,000 a year, which is 43% of that person and does the part of the job nobody enjoys. Three jobs at £2,950 a month is £35,400 a year and covers the work of roughly four people at £140,000. That is checkable in front of them.

None of this is a decision I can make for you. It is a recommendation with the evidence attached, and it should be written into `company/pricing.md` before the next document is built.

## Your first ten calls {#first-ten}

If you do nothing else with this book, do this.

**Pick one trade. Commercial cleaning.** Not because it pays the most, but
because the job is identical at every firm, the owner answers the phone, and
they already pay someone to sit there doing it. Ten calls, same words, one
afternoon.

**The call is five sentences.** Say them in this order and then stop talking.

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
The call
:::

"Hi, is that the owner? I'll be quick — I'm not selling software.

You invoice each site monthly and your office manager builds those invoices from
timesheets. Is that roughly right?

I build a small AI worker that does that one job — the timesheets, the site
sheets, the invoices — inside the system you already use.

It takes two weeks and costs £3,500. If it doesn't save the hours we agree, you
don't pay the second half.

Can I show you on your own last month's sheets, Thursday or Friday?"
:::

Then write down three things: how many sites they clean, who does the invoicing,
and what that person's day looks like. That is your research for call two.

**What you are listening for.** Not interest. A number. "We do about 140 sites."
"That takes her two days a month." A number is the start of a deal. Interest is
politeness.

**If they push back.** Three answers cover most of it.

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "We already have software for that." | "Then this sits on top of it. It does the typing between the systems, not the thinking." |
| "My office manager would worry." | "Good. She is the one who tells it what to do. It takes the boring half of her day, not her job." |
| "How do I know it works?" | "You don't yet. That is what the two weeks are for — it runs on last month's real sheets." |

**When they say no.** Ask one question: "What would have to be true for this to
be worth twenty minutes?" Then write it down word for word. Ten nos with reasons
are worth more than one polite maybe.

**After the call.** Send the one page. Not the deck. The page on the next spread.

## The one page to send {#one-page}

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
The claim chase · installed in two weeks
:::

**What it is.** One employee that works your chase list. It reads the file, works
out what is missing, writes to the person who owes it, and keeps chasing until
the file is complete. It logs every step with a date and a name.

**What goes in.** The documents you already hold — the case file, the evidence
list, the correspondence. Nothing is moved and nothing is replaced. It works
inside what you already use.

**What comes out.** A clean chase list every morning, the letters and emails
written and sent, and a weekly page showing what was chased, what came back, and
what is still outstanding.

**Who it suits.** Any firm paid when the file completes — a claim, a case, a
funding claim, a renewal. If chasing the paperwork is somebody's whole job, this
is that job.

:::

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
The terms
:::

**What it must never do.** It does not decide anything a person is required to
decide. It does not advise, approve or sign. Anything that looks like a
vulnerable customer, a complaint or a legal question goes to a named human and
waits there.

**The two-week test.** We run it on your last twenty completed files. If it does
not reproduce the work your team did, you keep your money.

**The price.** £3,500 for the install and the two-week test. £1,250 a month to
keep it running, cancel with a month's notice.

**To start.** Reply with one word — "chase" — and a time next week. We need one
hour and twenty redacted files.
:::

Say it the same way every time. The job can change — the renewal desk, the
funding claim, the post-completion pack — but the shape of the page does not.

Everything else in this book is for you. This page is for them. It replaces the
deck on a first approach, because 52 pages asks for an hour of someone's life
before they have any reason to give it.

## The next fourteen days {#fourteen-days}

**Days 1–2. Fix the front door.** Rotate the two exposed keys. Fix `www`, which currently shows a certificate warning to anyone who types it. Put one analytics tag on the site. Without these, every hour of outbound work lands on a broken page and you cannot see it.

**Day 3. Pick one job, not a sector.** Choose the single job you can demonstrate this week. My pick is the claim or case evidence chase, because it is the same in four of the eight trades and you already have the words for it.

**Day 4. Build the demo.** One live employee with its own email address, working on sample documents that look like theirs. Not a video. A thing they can email and get an answer from. The mail plumbing already exists on this machine.

**Day 5. Write one page.** The job, what goes in, what comes out, what it must never do, the two-week price, the date it starts. One page. No deck.

**Days 6–10. Ten calls a day from this book.** Open with the job, not the technology. Ask one question: "how many of these did your team get through last week?" Write down the number. That number is your evidence base.

**Days 11–12. Two follow-ups to everyone who gave you a number.** Not a reminder. A specific: "I built the thing on your last twenty files. Here it is."

**Days 13–14. Close one install.** £3,500, start date agreed, one job defined in writing. If nobody says yes, the job is wrong, not the market. Change the job, keep the list.

## What would make me wrong {#wrong}

- **If per-unit firms are already automated.** Some are. R&D consultancies in particular have bought software. Ask before you pitch.
- **If the buyer is never the person you named.** Titles lie. The name gets you to the switchboard, not to the decision.
- **If the install cannot be done in two weeks.** Then the job is too big and should be cut again, not sold harder.
- **If the monthly never lands.** Then you are running a project business, not a product one. That is a different company with different economics, and you should know which one you are building.
