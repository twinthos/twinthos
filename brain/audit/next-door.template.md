## Read this first {#read-this-first}

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
The one line
:::

**You are not selling AI. You are selling one finished job, on a date, for less than the person doing it now.**
:::

The last list was good research and a hard sell. Every firm on it has admin. Every one has also been pitched AI three times this year, so the call starts in a crowded room.

This book starts somewhere else.

It looks for firms that get paid **per completed piece of work** — per claim, per case, per renewal, per placement, per funding claim. In those firms, admin capacity is the ceiling on revenue. Slow admin means money that never arrives.

That changes what you are to the person on the phone. You are not the consultant asking them to shrink their own team. You are more capacity in a business that is paid by the unit. That is a promotion, not a threat.

Three things follow from it:

- The first step gets small. One job, two weeks, a fixed fee. One person can say yes.
- The pitch gets short. "You bill per claim. Your team can process this many a week. This makes it that many."
- The monthly number arrives after a result, not before one.

The rest of this book is the list, the words, and the order to call in.

## Why the last book stalls {#why-it-stalls}

Three reasons. Each one has a fix, and the fix is why this list is different.

### 1. You ask them to shrink their own team

The person who owns the admin queue is the person whose team gets smaller. Nobody gets promoted for that. They say "interesting" and nothing happens.

**Fix:** pick firms where more throughput means more revenue. Then the queue getting faster is their win, and they will fight for it internally.

### 2. The price needs a second signature

At £60,000 a year, an owner-managed firm has to discuss it. A discussion is a delay, and delays die.

**Fix:** make the first step one person's decision. One job, two weeks, £3,500. The monthly fee is a separate conversation held after the thing has already worked.

### 3. Nothing has a date on it

"Would you like this?" has no deadline. A diary with no date in it is a wish.

**Fix:** only call firms that already have a date. A renewal, a funding return, an audit, a claim window, a go-live.

## The five-minute test {#five-minute-test}

Answer all five before you dial. If you cannot, do not dial.

| # | Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What do they get paid for? | Per-unit firms have a reason to say yes |
| 2 | How many units a month? | This is the size of the prize, in their language |
| 3 | Who signs without asking anyone? | The name on the call sheet |
| 4 | What date forces it? | The reason the call happens this month |
| 5 | Can it run without patient data? | Keeps you out of the regulated queue |

A firm that passes all five is worth an hour. A firm that passes three is worth an email. A firm that passes two is a bookmark.

## Part one — the eight simple trades {#part-one}

These are the ones to call first. In each of them the job is obvious, the owner
decides alone, and nobody has to check with a compliance officer.

They also score higher than the harder trades on the one thing that matters this
month: you can explain the job in a single sentence, and the person on the phone
already knows they pay someone to do it.

{{SCORE_TABLE}}

### Food and drink wholesalers {#sector-wholesale}

Orders arrive by phone, email and WhatsApp all morning. Someone retypes them into
the order system. Miss one and you lose the sale. Take it wrong and you lose the
customer.

Every distributor has a person doing this. They all know it is the job. That
makes the conversation short.

{{TABLE:wholesale}}

### Plant and tool hire {#sector-planthire}

A machine goes out on a hire contract and comes back. Someone has to close the
contract, check for damage, charge for it and invoice.

Every day a machine sits off-hire and uncollected is money gone. The chase is
the job, and the chase is done by hand.

{{TABLE:planthire}}

### Accident repair centres {#sector-bodyshop}

Every repair runs the same loop: estimate, insurer approval, parts, photos,
invoice, chase the insurer. The insurer pays per repair, so a faster desk means
more cars out of the door.

The owner is in the workshop, not in the office. That is the gap you fill.

{{TABLE:bodyshop}}

### Commercial cleaning contractors {#sector-clean}

Every site needs a timesheet, an audit and a monthly invoice. The office manager
does all three by hand, between phone calls.

Payroll is their biggest cost, so every hour of admin is a cost they can see on
the profit line.

{{TABLE:clean}}

### Security guarding companies {#sector-security}

Officers work shifts across dozens of sites. Someone builds the rota, chases
missed clock-ins, watches licence expiry dates and invoices each site monthly.

One missed licence can lose a contract. That makes this admin a risk item, and
risk items get budget.

{{TABLE:security}}

### Event and AV contractors {#sector-events}

Each event is a quote, a kit list, a crew booking, a load-out and a damage list.
The season is short and everything happens at once.

A slow quote is a lost job. In August, a slow quote is two lost jobs.

{{TABLE:events}}

### Print and signage {#sector-print}

Every job is a quote, an artwork approval, a proof, and sometimes a reprint
because the customer changed their mind.

Chasing approval is the same email every time. It is also why jobs sit still
while the press is free.

{{TABLE:print}}

### Removals and storage {#sector-removals}

Every move is a survey, a quote, a booking, a crew sheet, a claim if something
breaks, and an invoice.

Most quotes are never followed up. That is money already earned and then
dropped on the floor.

{{TABLE:removals}}

## Part two — the harder trades {#part-two}

These were in the first draft. They are good businesses and they spend more per
client, but there is a gatekeeper between you and a yes: a compliance officer, a
regulator, or a finance director.

Nothing is wrong with them. They are just not where you start. Come back in month
two, when you can say "we already do this for six firms".

{{SCORE_TABLE_HARD}}

None of these appear in the last book. They are scored the same way as everything
else in this file, so the numbers compare directly. Ten is good, and on "simple"
ten means no regulator and no gatekeeper.

{{SCORE_TABLE}}

The important number: **the best new sector outscores the best old one.** Research and development claims work scores 8.15 against accountancy's 8.1. It is also a smaller, richer, faster-moving buyer.

### Research and development claims {#sector-randd}

The buyer writes a technical report for every claim. The report has the same shape every time. The fee per claim is large, so the cost of producing one is visible on the profit line. The firm is usually owner-managed with ten to eighty staff and no procurement function.

The date is real: a claim has a statutory window, and a missed notification loses the money outright. There is also an audit trail to defend afterwards.

{{TABLE:randd}}

### Mortgage and protection brokers {#sector-mortgage}

The broker is paid when the case completes, not when the advice is given. Every case is the same fourteen steps. Admin capacity decides how many complete in a month, and remortgage waves arrive whether the team is ready or not.

The line you never cross: the agent packages and chases. It does not advise. Write that into the job definition and the compliance officer stops being your enemy.

{{TABLE:mortgage}}

### Business energy and utilities consultancies {#sector-energy}

Commission per signed contract. A renewal diary is a list, and a missed renewal is lost commission. Letters of authority, supplier onboarding and invoice validation are paper jobs done at volume, and the firms are growing headcount to keep up.

Check one thing before you call: does the firm publish its supplier panel? The brokers with a published panel are the ones worth your time.

{{TABLE:energy}}

### IT resellers and managed service providers {#sector-it}

Margin per renewal, and the renewal date belongs to the vendor, not to them. Licence reconciliation is arithmetic nobody wants to do. They already buy services and they understand a monthly fee.

The risk is obvious: they could build it themselves. So do not sell them AI. Sell them the renewals desk without hiring two more people before the vendor's price rise lands.

{{TABLE:it}}

### Debt recovery and credit management {#sector-debt}

This is the purest version of the filter. The firm is paid a commission on money it recovers. Every extra letter, plan and claim form processed is income on the same job. Capacity is the business.

The caution is real too: anything touching a vulnerable customer needs a person to review it before it goes out. That is a design requirement. Say so before they do, and it becomes a reason to trust you.

{{TABLE:debt}}

### Franchisors {#sector-franchisors}

One small head office supports dozens of owner-operators. Every month brings the same royalty return, the same brand requests, the same compliance pack. One signature covers the whole network, and every franchisee sees the result.

The buyer is the support office, not the board. They are measured on how quickly a new franchisee becomes productive.

{{TABLE:franchisors}}

### Training and apprenticeship providers {#sector-training}

Funding is paid per learner per month, and it only lands if the evidence file is complete. A gap is a clawback, which makes this admin a money item rather than a cost item.

Margins are thin, so aim at the larger independents. Ask how many learners and what the funding mix is before you talk about a fee.

{{TABLE:training}}

### Chartered surveyors and valuation firms {#sector-surveyors}

A fee per instruction, and every instruction produces the same report from the same sections. Partner-led firms, no procurement, and a typing and admin pool that is always the constraint. Lender panel formats change, and every template has to follow.

{{TABLE:surveyors}}

## Beyond the UK {#beyond-uk}

Britain is a good place to learn the job and a small place to build a business. The same filter works in any market where someone is paid per completed item.

These are the markets to test after the first three UK clients, not before. Each carries a named person and a source so the list survives a check.

{{TABLE:international}}

Why these markets and not others: English-language contracts, a recognisable regulatory shape, and a strong culture of buying outsourced administration. Ireland and Australia are the easiest to run from a UK base because the time zone and the legal vocabulary are close.

## Who would buy the company {#acquirers}

A target list is not the only list worth having. Some organisations would rather own the capability than rent it.

Four kinds matter:

- **Business-process outsourcers.** They sell admin labour and they are being repriced by AI. Buying a working managed-agent capability is faster than building one.
- **IT services groups on a buy-and-build.** They already buy small firms and bolt them on.
- **Integrators and consultancies** with an AI practice and no delivery bench.
- **Vertical software vendors** whose customers are stuck on old desktop systems. They need an agent layer and cannot ship one quickly.

{{TABLE:acquirers}}

The last column is my judgement, not theirs. The five marked "start here" are
small enough to answer a cold email from a company of your size, and specific
enough to see what they would do with it. The three long shots would take six
months to reach a person who could say yes. Do not spend a week on them.

How to use this list without wasting a month: send one paragraph, not a deck. Say what the thing does, that it runs today, and that you are open to a conversation about partnership or acquisition. Nothing else.

## Who would back you {#investors}

You do not need money to land the first client. You need it to survive the gap between the first client and the tenth. Two different asks:

**A co-founder.** The useful profile is someone who has sold and delivered agency work before. They bring the sales habit and the delivery discipline. They do not need to be technical.

**An operator-angel.** Someone who owns or has sold a services business, who can open doors to the exact buyer described in this book, and who will not ask for a board seat at pre-revenue.

{{TABLE:investors}}

Three of these names are already on the cap table of your closest competitor,
and that is the most useful thing in this section.

**Piers Linney** runs Implement AI — managed digital workers sold into UK SMEs.
**SFC Capital** wrote its pre-seed cheque, and **Nigel Wray** led its £1.3m.
So the three closest fits on this list have already funded the company you would
be described as a copy of. Do not send them a pitch deck. Send them one line:
"you backed Implement AI, I am building the same thing one step further down the
market, in these eight trades — worth twenty minutes?" That is either a partner,
an acquirer, or a very fast no. All three outcomes are useful.

Everyone else on the list is a genuine pre-seed conversation, and a fund will ask
about retention before it asks about the market. The honest line is "one install,
no retainers yet, here is the list and the price".

The honest position to take into those conversations: pre-revenue, one founder, a clear job, a price that works and a list. Do not describe a platform. Describe the first ten clients and the margin on each.

## The price {#price}

Four numbers exist in the current material and they do not agree: £750 a month, a £1,500 pilot, £5,000 a month, £60,000 a year. `company/pricing.md` contains none of the £1,500. A buyer cannot act on a document that contradicts itself, and neither can you.

Here is what the evidence outside your own files says.

| Source | What it charges | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Nick Vasilescu, first client, 2025 | $3,500 for one agent, sold in week one | One job, one install, paid up front |
| Belgian web designer, Aug 2026 | €2,750 install, €267 a month | Small monthly is enough if the install is paid |
| Nick's two working models, Aug 2026 | $15k up front then $2–3k a month, or $5k flat | The up-front fee is the part clients accept |
| UK managed-agent band, Oct 2026 | £750–£2,000 a month per role | £5,000 for one role is outside the band |

The pattern across all four: **the install is paid, the monthly is modest, and the job is narrow.** Your £5,000 monthly for one role sits above every UK comparable. Your £1,500 pilot sits below what operators charge for an install.

A ladder that fits both the evidence and your own test prices:

| Step | Price | What it is | Who can sign it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The install | £3,500 | One job, two weeks, measured against today's numbers | One person |
| One job, running | £1,250 a month | The same job, maintained, with a monthly evidence page | One person |
| Three jobs | £2,950 a month | Three defined jobs on one brain | One person, still under most limits |
| The audit | £999 | Two days, a written map of where the hours go | Anyone |

The arithmetic you can say out loud: one admin on £28,000 costs about £35,000 loaded. One job at £1,250 a month is £15,000 a year, which is 43% of that person and does the part of the job nobody enjoys. Three jobs at £2,950 a month is £35,400 a year and covers the work of roughly four people at £140,000. That is checkable in front of them.

None of this is a decision I can make for you. It is a recommendation with the evidence attached, and it should be written into `company/pricing.md` before the next document is built.

## Your first ten calls {#first-ten}

If you do nothing else with this book, do this.

**Pick one trade. Commercial cleaning.** Not because it pays the most, but
because the job is identical at every firm, the owner answers the phone, and
they already pay someone to sit there doing it. Ten calls, same words, one
afternoon.

**The call is five sentences.** Say them in this order and then stop talking.

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
The call
:::

"Hi, is that the owner? I'll be quick — I'm not selling software.

You invoice each site monthly and your office manager builds those invoices from
timesheets. Is that roughly right?

I build a small AI worker that does that one job — the timesheets, the site
sheets, the invoices — inside the system you already use.

It takes two weeks and costs £3,500. If it doesn't save the hours we agree, you
don't pay the second half.

Can I show you on your own last month's sheets, Thursday or Friday?"
:::

Then write down three things: how many sites they clean, who does the invoicing,
and what that person's day looks like. That is your research for call two.

**What you are listening for.** Not interest. A number. "We do about 140 sites."
"That takes her two days a month." A number is the start of a deal. Interest is
politeness.

**If they push back.** Three answers cover most of it.

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "We already have software for that." | "Then this sits on top of it. It does the typing between the systems, not the thinking." |
| "My office manager would worry." | "Good. She is the one who tells it what to do. It takes the boring half of her day, not her job." |
| "How do I know it works?" | "You don't yet. That is what the two weeks are for — it runs on last month's real sheets." |

**When they say no.** Ask one question: "What would have to be true for this to
be worth twenty minutes?" Then write it down word for word. Ten nos with reasons
are worth more than one polite maybe.

**After the call.** Send the one page. Not the deck. The page on the next spread.

## The one page to send {#one-page}

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
The claim chase · installed in two weeks
:::

**What it is.** One employee that works your chase list. It reads the file, works
out what is missing, writes to the person who owes it, and keeps chasing until
the file is complete. It logs every step with a date and a name.

**What goes in.** The documents you already hold — the case file, the evidence
list, the correspondence. Nothing is moved and nothing is replaced. It works
inside what you already use.

**What comes out.** A clean chase list every morning, the letters and emails
written and sent, and a weekly page showing what was chased, what came back, and
what is still outstanding.

**Who it suits.** Any firm paid when the file completes — a claim, a case, a
funding claim, a renewal. If chasing the paperwork is somebody's whole job, this
is that job.

:::

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
The terms
:::

**What it must never do.** It does not decide anything a person is required to
decide. It does not advise, approve or sign. Anything that looks like a
vulnerable customer, a complaint or a legal question goes to a named human and
waits there.

**The two-week test.** We run it on your last twenty completed files. If it does
not reproduce the work your team did, you keep your money.

**The price.** £3,500 for the install and the two-week test. £1,250 a month to
keep it running, cancel with a month's notice.

**To start.** Reply with one word — "chase" — and a time next week. We need one
hour and twenty redacted files.
:::

Say it the same way every time. The job can change — the renewal desk, the
funding claim, the post-completion pack — but the shape of the page does not.

Everything else in this book is for you. This page is for them. It replaces the
deck on a first approach, because 52 pages asks for an hour of someone's life
before they have any reason to give it.

## The next fourteen days {#fourteen-days}

This is the part that matters. The list is worth nothing on its own.

**Days 1–2. Fix the front door.** Rotate the two exposed keys. Fix `www`, which currently shows a certificate warning to anyone who types it. Put one analytics tag on the site. Without these, every hour of outbound work lands on a broken page and you cannot see it.

**Day 3. Pick one job, not a sector.** Choose the single job you can demonstrate this week. My pick is the claim or case evidence chase, because it is the same in four of the eight trades and you already have the words for it.

**Day 4. Build the demo.** One live employee with its own email address, working on sample documents that look like theirs. Not a video. A thing they can email and get an answer from. The mail plumbing already exists on this machine.

**Day 5. Write one page.** The job, what goes in, what comes out, what it must never do, the two-week price, the date it starts. One page. No deck.

**Days 6–10. Ten calls a day from this book.** Open with the job, not the technology. Ask one question: "how many of these did your team get through last week?" Write down the number. That number is your evidence base.

**Days 11–12. Two follow-ups to everyone who gave you a number.** Not a reminder. A specific: "I built the thing on your last twenty files. Here it is."

**Days 13–14. Close one install.** £3,500, start date agreed, one job defined in writing. If nobody says yes, the job is wrong, not the market. Change the job, keep the list.

What success looks like on day 14

One paid install at £3,500, three written job definitions from real calls, and a number from ten firms about how many items their team processes in a week. Anything less means the job was too broad.

## What would make me wrong {#wrong}

- **If per-unit firms are already automated.** Some are. R&D consultancies in particular have bought software. Ask before you pitch.
- **If the buyer is never the person you named.** Titles lie. The name gets you to the switchboard, not to the decision.
- **If the install cannot be done in two weeks.** Then the job is too big and should be cut again, not sold harder.
- **If the monthly never lands.** Then you are running a project business, not a product one. That is a different company with different economics, and you should know which one you are building.
