## What we are actually selling

A **digital employee** has three parts, and none of them is a chatbot.

**The Brain.** The client's rules, prices, tone, exceptions and past decisions, held in one place and isolated per client. It does not reset between conversations, and it does not invent a memory to fill a gap.

**The Employee.** One job with a written definition: what goes in, what comes out, what it must never do, when it stops and asks a human. That definition is the product. A general assistant has no job, which is why nobody can be accountable for what it produces.

**The Desk.** It works through the screens the client already owns — the case management system, the broking platform, the payroll software, the insurer portal, Outlook. No API, no migration, no two-year integration project. The first job is live in about two hours, which is the only reason a two-week pilot is credible.

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
The distinction that matters in the room
:::

ChatGPT, Grok, Muse and Claude are general assistants sold per seat. No knowledge of the firm, no permissions, no audit trail, no escalation rule, and nobody accountable when the output is wrong. That is a different product, not a cheaper one. The honest sentence is: the model is fuel, and it is swappable. The value is the memory, the job definition, the permissions and the verification around it.
:::

## How to price it without arguing

Never price against a subscription. Price against the salary the work currently sits on, because that is the budget line the client already has.

One UK admin on £28,000, loaded at 25% for employer NI, pension and overhead, costs **£35,000 a year** — £2,917 a month, £17.95 an hour, for about 1,950 working hours. That is the comparator. A £750-a-month Foundation workflow is 26% of one hire. A £5,000-a-month desk is £60,000 a year and has to replace roughly 1.7 salaries, or add £60,000 of revenue, to make sense.

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
Say this before they do
:::

"Compare it to the person doing it now, not to a software licence. This is the cost of one and a half of those people, and it does not sleep, does not forget and leaves an audit trail."
:::

The market check is uncomfortable and worth knowing: the UK managed-agent band sits at **£750–£2,000 a month per role** (Ajairu from £750, Tenaxity £1,250, Locate AI £2,000 flat). At £5,000 for a single role, a finance director has three published comparables that undercut us by 60–85%. Two ways to answer that. Either sell the £5,000 as a **three-role bundle** — the human comparator is two VA seats at £10,352 a month, so it is a 52% discount to human cover — or move the single-role entry price down and let the bundle carry the margin. That is a decision for Tyson, not for a deck. Until it is made, no document in this book states a price as final.

::: {.box .ok}
::: {.label}
The anchor that needs no trust
:::

Big Four published G-Cloud day rates run **£450 to £2,750 a day** (Deloitte, PwC UK, KPMG all publish cards). One month of any Twinthos desk is cheaper than two days of a Big Four consultant, and the client can check that themselves in ten seconds. Tenhaw charges **£44,000** for an agentic readiness audit and publishes it, which proves buyers will pay for a scoped first stage.
:::

## The register

Every firm below was verified on 21 October 2026. The evidence for each is its own website, its filed accounts at Companies House, or a vacancy it was advertising that day. Where a firm was advertising the exact role the work sits on, that is the strongest possible signal: the cost is already budgeted and the queue is already too long.

A word on health, because it matters. A competitor's view is that UK and US healthcare are harder industries to sell into, and the caution is right for the regulated core: patient data is special-category under UK GDPR Article 9, providers answer to CQC, NHS work runs through DSPT and DTAC, and a small vendor without those artefacts stalls in procurement. Section six of this book keeps the healthcare names but ranks them last and states the gate in writing. The sectors to work first are the ones where the data is commercially sensitive but not statutorily special, and where one operations director signs.

## Insurance — 12 firms {#sec-insurance}

::: {.kick}
Section two
:::

::: {.lead}
Renewals stopped being cheap the day the pricing rules changed. That switch is the whole pitch, and it is dated and checkable.
:::

Since the FCA's general insurance pricing practices rules (PS21/11) a renewal price cannot exceed the equivalent new business price. Renewals now carry close to new-business admin: a UK broker workflow study puts renewal re-marketing at **3–5 hours per handler per week**, spiking in renewal season. On top of that, ICOBS requires a demands-and-needs statement, evidenced, for every policy sold or renewed — 2–4 hours per handler per week drafting, checking and filing. Mid-term adjustments arrive all week rather than in batches, and each one needs a premium recalculation, an endorsement and an insurer confirmation chased by email.

The judgement has to stay with a qualified person under ICOBS and Consumer Duty. So the promise is never "replaces the broker". It is "removes the blank page and the re-keying, and stops for approval before anything reaches a client".

::: {.box}
::: {.label}
The money
:::

A fully loaded account handler at £30 an hour, 45 working weeks, 20 handlers on the same repeating job: renewal re-marketing at 4 hours each is **£108,000 a year**; demands-and-needs at 3 hours is **£81,000**; MTA processing at 2.5 hours is **£67,500**; first-notification triage and chasing at 4.5 hours is **£121,500**. One desk at £60,000 covers three of those budgets at once. Per handler, 4 hours a week of renewal admin costs £5,400 a year, so the £1,500 pilot only has to return about 50 hours to break even.
:::

## Healthcare — 15 firms {#sec-healthcare}

::: {.kick}
Section six — ranked last on purpose
:::

::: {.lead}
The caution is correct for the regulated core. Patient data is special-category, the gatekeepers are institutional, and a small vendor without the artefacts stalls in procurement. The names stay because the private end of the market is reachable, but they are not where the first money comes from.
:::

UK GDPR Article 9 makes health data special-category: it needs an Article 9 condition, a DPIA, and a lawful basis the provider's own DPO signs. NHS-facing work adds DSPT and DTAC expectations, and CQC-registered providers must be able to produce evidence against its single assessment framework on demand. A vendor turning up with a promising demo and no IG artefacts will lose six months to that queue.

The jobs themselves are real and countable: recall lists segmented by NICE CG19 intervals rather than blasted, DNA rebooking, PMI authorisation chasing between the insurer portal and the consultant diary, e-Referral queues, and rota cover at 200–500 sites. In private dentistry, physiotherapy, aesthetics and veterinary work, the payer is the patient or an insurer rather than the state, which removes most of the procurement weight.

If a healthcare deal is taken on, four things go in the first email, in writing: the agent works under the client's own DSPT and DPIA; there is no bulk export, screen-level work only; special-category data is processed only on the client's lawful basis with DPO sign-off; and no client data is used for model training.

## The five objections, answered

| What they say | What is true |
|---|---|
| "We already have Copilot." | Copilot drafts text inside a document. It does not log into the case management system, chase a third party, or close a file. Ask them what Copilot finished last week without a human driving it. |
| "Our system has no API." | That is why this works. The desk drives the screens a person drives. No API, no migration, no vendor project — that is a two-hour deployment, not a two-year one. |
| "Who is accountable when it is wrong?" | A named human, per desk, in writing. Every action is logged and reversible, and anything consequential stops for approval. That is a stronger position than a member of staff who leaves no trail. |
| "It will make mistakes." | Yes. So the job is scoped to work where a mistake is caught by a checkable rule, and the pilot measures the error rate before anyone commits. |
| "We tried RPA and it broke." | RPA breaks because it scripts a fixed path. This reads the screen and adapts, and it escalates instead of failing silently. The pilot is where you prove that on your own material. |
