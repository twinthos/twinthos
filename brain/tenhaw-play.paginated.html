The Tenhaw play.
Can Twinthos run it?

A one-man London consultancy prints £44,000 on its website and sells a four-week audit. The price is real. The question is what sits behind it, and which parts survive the trip down to Twinthos’s buyer.

What Tenhaw actually is

Tenhaw Ltd, company number 12735685, incorporated 10 July 2020. One officer: James Anthony Rooney, born November 1991. The registered office moved on 5 October 2026 from a residential address in Letchworth to a serviced building on Scrutton Street, Shoreditch.

The accounts made up to 31 July 2025 record the average monthly number of employees, including the director, as one.

So the “forward-deployed teams” and “no bench in the price” on the homepage are literally true and commercially narrow: one principal, plus associates brought in per engagement, sold at senior day rates.

Read the logos carefully

HSBC, Microsoft, Sky, F1, Discovery, Greggs and Anglo American are career history, not Tenhaw contracts. The team page says it plainly: “James’s experience spans employed and contract roles as well as Tenhaw engagements.” Any competitor copying the logo wall without the CV behind it is copying the weakest part of the site.

Anatomy of the £44,000

Thirty-four people-days across four weeks: five from the partner, twenty from a senior operator, nine from a build engineer.

£1,294

Published blended cost per person-day. × 34 days = £43,996.

The fee is the rate card at list. No discount, no premium, no loss-leader. Tenhaw states the same thing in its own FAQ: the blended rate “sits between our £1,250 senior practitioner rate and £1,560 partner rate”, and the fixed fee means “we absorb any additional time needed to deliver it”.

What the buyer keeps at the end of four weeks:

  • Two or three working prototypes, built in the client’s own tenancy against real data, each with measured accuracy and an estimated run cost
  • A heat map of candidates ranked by expected return and feasibility
  • A constraint register covering data, platform, risk and regulation
  • A decision inventory: which decisions an agent takes, which stay with a person
  • A costed plan in three-month increments, capped at twelve months
  • An investment case in currency with assumptions attached
  • A list of what to leave out, and why
  • Code in their repositories, owned on payment

Where the money actually is

The number that explains the price

The audit is 5% of the follow-on. Tenhaw’s own published year-one example: £44,000 audit plus eleven months of build team = £814,000–£979,000. The audit is a qualification gate on a £900,000 programme, not a product.

That is the whole trick. £44,000 is cheap to a board that has already decided it will spend seven figures, and it is a rounding error against the cost of starting the wrong programme. The audit is priced at the altitude of the decision it supports.

The same arithmetic explains why the number looks absurd anywhere else. A recruitment agency turning over two million pounds has no nine-hundred-thousand-pound programme for the audit to gate. Carry the identical four-week shape down to that buyer and the ratio dictates a fee near three thousand pounds, because at forty-four thousand the audit stops being the doorway and becomes the entire engagement.

There is a second thing the ratio buys, and it is not obvious from the price list: permission to say no. When the audit is one per cent of the prize, the seller needs every deal. When it is five per cent of a decision the buyer has already funded, the seller can walk away from a bad fit without losing the year — and walking away is what makes the publish-everything posture credible.

The honest conclusion

The £44,000 is a published price, not evidence of a market. Nothing in the public record shows a queue of buyers paying it. What the site shows is a competent operator building the asset that makes the price credible: 12 case studies, about 1,440 questions answered in public, a published method, published rate card and published insurance levels.

This matters because the tempting read — “a company charges £44,000, so buyers will pay £44,000” — is the same error as the block management deck: inferring demand from a number rather than from a transaction.

The seven moves worth copying

  1. Print the price. It ends the “how much?” email chain before it starts and filters tyre-kickers without a call.
  2. Make the entry rung fixed-price and bounded. Four weeks, defined deliverables, a decision at the end. Not an open retainer dressed as an audit.
  3. Publish the method free. Tenhaw gives away “The Tenhaw Way” and seventeen how-to guides, and says “You may not need us.” What the client buys is altitude — someone senior carrying the change across departments — not information.
  4. Derive prices from a published rate card. A buyer who can check the arithmetic stops negotiating the number and starts comparing the scope.
  5. Build the question surface. About 1,440 answered questions on one site is a search and AI-answer asset that works while the principal sleeps.
  6. Sell the exit. No minimum term, 30 days’ notice either way, code in the client’s repositories, deliverables owned on payment. Removing lock-in raises the price a buyer will accept, because the risk moves to the seller.
  7. Name the person. One named partner accountable for every engagement, with a real escalation route, is the whole differentiator against a Big Four pyramid.

What does not survive the trip downmarket

  • The £44,000. It needs a board that already has programme budget. A £2m recruitment agency does not have a £900k follow-on for the audit to be 5% of.
  • The Big Four anchor. Tenhaw’s 1.3×–2.3× comparison against published G-Cloud day rates only lands because the CV behind it is HSBC and Microsoft.
  • “No bench” at senior rates. Works at enterprise. At SMB, the buyer is comparing against a £22,000 administrator, not against PA Consulting.
  • Twelve case studies. Tenhaw has a decade of delivery history to draw on. Twinthos has to build the first three.

The Twinthos version

The transferable rule is the ratio, not the number: the audit should be about 5% of the year-one follow-on.

At £2,950 the audit is 4.9% of the follow-on — the same relationship Tenhaw runs at a hundred times the altitude. It also makes the £999 diagnostic look like what it is: a paid triage, not the product.

Recommendation, not fact

These prices are a recommendation from the ratio, not an adopted decision. The real constraint is what the market will bear at that buyer size, and only conversations settle that.

Thirty days

  1. Week one — print the ladder. Replace the enquiries-first pricing page with three rungs and a real number on each. No “contact us for pricing.”
  2. Week two — productise the audit. One page: the six things the buyer keeps, the days it takes, the fixed fee, what happens if they stop in week one.
  3. Week three — build the question surface. One hundred real questions from real calls, each answered on its own page, each linked from an index. This is the asset that compounds.
  4. Week four — sell ten. The audit goes to ten named firms from the target register, at £2,950, with the deliverable list in the first email.
