The verdict

Killed

Block management at £5,000 a month. The fee exceeds the buyer’s entire pre-tax profit, the incumbent shipped the product two weeks ago, and the law does not say what I said it says.

Survives — for a different reason

Recruitment. Not because of admin volume. Because of profit per employee: £160,200 of fee income per consultant against roughly £6,000 of profit per block manager. The comparison is on page 5.

The deeper error: I chose the sector by how much admin it had, not by whether the client could afford the fee.

Error one — I priced above their profit

Line Value
1,000 units × £300 fee £300,000
Pre-tax profit at 10% £30,000
Foundation tier, £2,500/mo £30,000/yr — 100% of profit
Brain + employee, £5,000/mo £60,000/yr — 200% of profit

Both test tiers land above the whole profit line. At the smallest tier the fee equals 100% of pre-tax profit; at the tier the recommendation actually named, it is double. No managing director signs either.

Error two — I inverted a number

I wrote that 1.23m units sat under ARMA members. The source says the opposite.

“…just 300 are members of ARMA. That means around 1.23 million residential units are managed by non-ARMA firms.”

— blockmanagementuk.ltd, ArticleID=139 (2020)

1.23m is the non-ARMA book. The figure is also six years old, and the 4,100 firm count is a modelled estimate, not a register.

Error three — the law does not say that

I wrote that a managing agent can be the principal accountable person. It normally cannot. The accountable person is the freeholder, the RMC, the RTM company or the local authority; an agent acts on behalf of them.

“You can delegate the work; you cannot delegate the duty.”

The agent produces the evidence. The client signs it off. The “legal defensibility” pitch fails in most leasehold structures, and a competent client would correct it in the first meeting.

Errors four and five — the incumbent, and the 8%

Error four — the incumbent shipped it

MRI, the system of record for block management, launched Agora Orchestrator on 6 October 2026. First client: James Andrew Residential, 6,500 homes, automating accounting, document processing and maintenance triage. I was selling agentic automation into a market where the software vendor already gives it away, bundled, inside the platform the client already pays for.

Error five — “same software” was 8%

8% of residential managing agents sit on the stack I called universal: 70 ARMA members out of roughly 870 firms. The whole argument rested on “one build works everywhere”. Their own number says one build works at one firm in twelve.

The correction — ask what the role earns

Test Right?
“How much admin do they have?” Wrong
“What does that role cost?” Wrong
“How much profit per head?” Right

Admin volume is a pain measure. Profit per head is a payment measure. Only one of them pays your invoice.

The number that settles it

37%

Of one recruiter’s annual output pays a £60,000 fee. The same fee is 1,000% of one block manager’s profit. That is the entire audit in one comparison.

Hays, year to 30 June 2025: £972.4m of net fees from 6,070 consultants — £160,200 of fee income generated per consultant, per year.

Sector Per head
Recruitment — fees generated £160,200
Block management — profit generated ≈ £6,000

Hays plc preliminary results FY2025, via Carson & Gray. The £6,000 is my estimate: £300k revenue × 10% PBT ÷ 5 staff.

Corrected pricing — price the desk, not the person

The digital employee does not serve one recruiter. It serves a desk: 20–30 consultants all doing the same repeating tasks.

What it covers Fee
One workflow, one branch £1,500 pilot
A full desk, 20–30 consultants £5,000/mo
Multi-branch group £1,200/branch

£5,000/mo against 25 consultants is £2,400 a year each — about 1.5% of the fee income that consultant generates. That is a defensible invoice.

Test prices, not market rates: Foundation £2,500 setup + £750/mo; Brain + digital employee from £5,000/mo; Operations Audit £999. Unvalidated — confirm before quoting a client.

Direction

  1. Drop block management — not forever, until you have a reference client.
  2. Run the profit-per-head test on any sector before writing another word about it.
  3. Call Hurley and Hipkiss. Both named, both reachable, both in a sector that can afford £5,000.
  4. Sell one desk, not one task. The pilot proves it; the desk pays for it.
  5. Never cite your own pricing file as evidence again. It is a test price, not a fact.
