14 questions to ask before you buy a managed AI agent

A short list to take into the meeting. Every question has an answer you want and a reply that should worry you. Hear two worrying replies and you walk.

The short answer

Ask what the job is, in writing. Ask where its memory lives and who can correct it. Ask what it is never allowed to do without a person, and who is accountable when it is wrong. Then ask what you own if you leave.

Start with the job, not the demo

**What exactly is the job, written down?** You want a document with the inputs, the outputs, the things it must never do, and when it hands the work to someone. Ask to see it before the demo. A supplier who cannot produce it has not built a product. They have built a conversation.

**How often does that job happen, and who does it now?** You want a frequency and a name. If nobody in your business does that job today, it is not a job. It is a hope.

**What number proves it worked?** Agree one number before you start, small enough to check by hand on your own material. Better efficiency is not a number.

What they sayWhat it usually means
"It is basically ChatGPT with our wrapper."No job, no permissions, no log. You are buying a seat with a logo on it.
"Our memory is the conversation history."It forgets by design. Every session starts from zero.
"It learns automatically, so there is nothing to maintain."Nobody can correct it, so nobody can trust it.
"We will need an API or a data migration."You have bought an integration project with a change programme attached.
"We have clients you can speak to," then silence.Either the clients are not real or the permission is not. Both matter.

Ask where it keeps what it learns

**Where does what it learns about our business live?** The answer should be a named store, kept separate for each client, with an owner inside your organisation. If the answer is conversation history, it forgets by design and every session starts from zero.

**What does it do when it does not know something?** The right answer is that it says so, asks, or passes the job to a person. The worrying one is an agent that fills the gap with a confident sentence.

**How is the memory corrected when it is wrong?** A named person should be able to edit it and have the correction stay. If corrections last one conversation, there is no memory.

It should never hold your master login

**Which systems does it touch, and with whose login?** Your accounts, your permissions, limited to the job. Never a shared account that can see everything.

**What can it read that it does not need?** Nothing, and that should be visible in the setup rather than promised in a call. Then ask: does our software have to change first? If it needs an API or a data migration and you do not have one, you have bought an integration project with a two year horizon. A managed agent should work through the screens your people already use.

Someone by name must be able to stop it

**What can it never do without a person?** You want a written list. Anything irreversible, anything that reaches a customer or a regulator, anything above a value you set.

**Who is accountable when the output is wrong?** A named person in your business, with the authority to stop the job. The model is not an answer.

**Show me the log of one finished job, end to end.** What it touched, in what order, what it changed, and when. This is the hardest thing to fake and the fastest way to judge the design. Then ask how fast it escalates when it stops, and to whom.

Ask what three years cost, and how you get out

**What does three years cost, not just the monthly fee?** Definition, running, oversight, failure and exit, on one page.

**How much of someone's day will the approvals take?** Half an hour or less, done in one batch, is fine. Two hours of checking means the saving has been moved, not made.

**What do we own at the end, and can we run the job elsewhere?** The right answer is yes to all three parts: the job definition, the memory and the log, in a format they can name. If the value sits in their model rather than in your memory, you are renting.

What this means for you

Pick one job and put one number on it. Send the questions in writing before the call, then use the call to test the answers. Score a point for anything you can verify and nothing for an adjective. Last question: what would you tell us not to buy? A supplier who can name the jobs they are wrong for has a real product.

Questions people actually ask

How many of these should a supplier answer on the first call?

Most of them. The job, the memory, the access and the accountability answers are the product, so a serious supplier gives them without preparation. Pricing detail and questions about your own systems can wait for a follow-up.

What if they have no clients we can speak to?

Ask what went wrong in their first month. A supplier with no paid clients can still be credible if they say so plainly and offer a short pilot measured on your own material. One who invents a case study cannot be trusted about anything else.

What single answer should end the meeting?

Two red flags, not one. It is basically ChatGPT with our wrapper, or our memory is the conversation history. Both mean no job, no permissions and no log. One is a bad call. Two is a pattern.
