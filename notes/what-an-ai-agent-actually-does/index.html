What an AI agent actually does when you give it a computer

Browser tools, file systems, calendars. The vendor demos show agents finishing tasks in minutes. The independent research is more cautious, and more useful.

The short version

  • An agent is a model that decides and acts, not one that only answers. It can call tools, read what comes back, and decide what to do next.
  • In 2024 and 2025, every major lab shipped a public agent: Anthropic Computer Use, OpenAI Operator, Google Astra, Salesforce Agentforce. They work on screen, in browsers, in CRMs.
  • METR's March 2025 study found the time horizon of successful AI tasks roughly doubles every seven months. The headline number for top models was about 50 minutes, with high variance.
  • Independent studies (Anthropic Economic Index, 2025) put the share of real Claude conversations that are agentic at around 5%. Most use is still single-turn Q&A.
  • Reliability, not capability, is the real ceiling. Agents that succeed 80% of the time still fail one in five; in business workflows that is the difference between a tool and a babysitter.

In 2024 and 2025, every major lab shipped an agent. Anthropic released Computer Use in public beta on 22 October 2024. OpenAI launched Operator in research preview on 23 January 2025. Google showed Project Astra, Salesforce launched Agentforce, Microsoft built agents into Copilot. Each demo showed the same kind of thing: a model that can see a screen, move a mouse, type a query, read the result, and decide what to do next.[1][2]

What an agent is, and what it isn't

A model answers a question. An agent finishes a job. The difference is the loop: an agent calls a tool, observes the output, and decides whether to stop, retry, or try something else. Anthropic's documentation puts it plainly: Computer Use is the Claude model with a screen, a keyboard and a cursor.[1]

This sounds like a small technical detail. It is not. Most AI failures in business workflows are not model failures. The model gives a reasonable answer to a reasonable question. The failure is that the workflow needed a sequence of calls, with the next one depending on what the last one returned. That is what agents do, and what plain chat does not.

What the demos show

Vendor demos tend to show a short, well-defined task on a clean system: book a restaurant, refund a customer, fill a form, scrape a page. The agent sees the screen, plans a few steps, takes them, and reports back. The result is short, the failure modes are hidden, and the screen is not the messy one your team works in.

The Anthropic launch page for Computer Use is honest about this. Its example tasks are use a computer to do the same things people do, and its own safety write-up flags that long-horizon, open-ended tasks remain unreliable.[1] OpenAI's Operator system card adds a similar caveat: Operator succeeds in 87.5% of web-based tasks in its own evaluation, but the tasks are short and well-scoped.[2]

What the measurements show

The most useful independent measurement so far is METR's Measuring the Impact of Post-Training on Task Length, published in March 2025.[3] METR asked models to complete real tasks from open-source repositories, scored how long the tasks would take a human, and watched how often the model finished one. The headline finding: the time horizon of successful AI tasks has been roughly doubling every seven months. The current frontier sits around 50 minutes, with very wide variance.

That is a striking curve. It is also a soft ceiling for a particular kind of work. A 50-minute horizon covers a refund, a booking change, a small research task. It does not cover close the books for the month or onboard the new supplier. Those need an hour of unbroken focus, a half-dozen tools, and a person to sign off.

The other useful number is from the Anthropic Economic Index, which Anthropic began publishing in February 2025.[4] It analyses millions of real Claude conversations. In its first reports, the share of conversations that are agentic (more than one tool call, or a sequence of decisions) sits around 5%. The other 95% is single-turn Q&A, writing help, or brainstorming. People are using agents. They are not yet using them for most of their work.

Where agents break

Four failure modes show up in the field reports, in roughly this order of cost.

  • Tool errors compound. An agent that is 95% reliable on each step is only 60% reliable over ten steps. Most business workflows have more than ten steps, and any one of them can be flaky. A booking that goes through but emails the wrong address is worse than a booking that fails loudly.
  • Auth breaks flows. A login that needs a one-time code, a bank that needs a hardware key, a CRM that needs a session refresh. These are not edge cases. They are the places where the human adds value.
  • State is invisible. A model that just spent 20 minutes on a refund does not necessarily know which refund it is on. The next time it sees a similar screen, it may redo the work. A human does not, because humans remember.
  • Recovery is the test. A 100% reliable agent that does not notice it has gone wrong is worse than a 90% reliable agent that asks. Vendors are working on this. It is the part of the field that will decide whether agents become office software or stay as research previews.

The 5% rule, and why it matters

If 5% of real work is agent-shaped today, the question is not whether agents will replace most workers. It is whether that 5% will grow at the rate METR's curve suggests, and which parts of your operation are inside it. A useful internal test: take one task your team repeats each week, write down the steps and the tools, and ask whether a careful person could hand it to a model that can see a screen. If yes, that is a candidate. If no, you are not yet in the curve.

What to try first, in your own work

  1. Pick one task that runs at least weekly, costs you more in time than in money, and has a clear definition of done. Examples: chasing a missing invoice, scheduling a recurring call, summarising a daily inbox.
  2. Run it three times by hand, with a stopwatch. Note the tools, the screens, the steps. If you cannot write the steps down, the agent will not be able to do them either.
  3. Try it with a sandboxed agent (Anthropic Computer Use, OpenAI Operator, or a local model with a browser tool). Compare time and outcome against your manual run. Count every step where you had to step in.
  4. If the agent is faster AND finishes without you stepping in, automate it. If it is faster but you are stepping in, you are the system. You have not saved time; you have moved it.

An agent is not magic, and the people selling it are not neutral. The independent measurements say the curve is real. They also say the curve is short, the ceiling is reliability, and most work is still outside it. The honest version of AI agents in 2026 is a tool that handles a small, well-defined slice of your week, and asks you when it gets stuck.

Common questions

What is an AI agent?

A model that decides and acts, not one that only answers. It can call tools, observe results, and decide what to do next. The clearest working definition comes from Anthropic's Computer Use launch in October 2024: the model with a screen, a keyboard and a cursor.

How long can an AI agent work without help?

About 50 minutes for the best current models on real tasks, according to METR's March 2025 study. The frontier has been roughly doubling every seven months. The same study found high variance, so a 50-minute mean hides a long tail of failures.

What share of real AI use is agentic?

Around 5% of real conversations on Claude in early 2025, per the Anthropic Economic Index. Most use is still single-turn Q&A, writing help or brainstorming.

Are AI agents safe to use in business?

Anthropic, OpenAI and Google have all published system cards for their agent products. They describe categories of misuse and rate the risk. For most routine office work, the risk is reliability rather than safety: an agent that gets things 80% right still needs a person to check the 20%.
