Everyone is shipping an “AI agent” this month. Almost none of them are agents.

Google, OpenAI, Meta and Anthropic all released something called an agent in October 2026. The word now covers everything from a chatbot to a worker with its own computer. Here is what actually separates the two — and why it decides whether your business gets a colleague or a demo.

The short version

  • In a single month, Google (14 Oct), OpenAI (12 Oct), Meta (29 Sep) and Anthropic all shipped things marketed as agents — but they mean very different things by the word.
  • A true agent acts in other systems on your behalf, holds state across a task, and keeps going after you close the conversation. A chatbot answers; an agent finishes.
  • The 2026 Remote Labour Index found the best agent completed just 2.5% of 240 real freelance projects end-to-end — and failed mostly on client-facing judgment work, not on structured tasks.
  • The gap between demo and colleague is not intelligence. It is permissions, memory, and a drawn line saying where the software may act and where a person decides.
  • The number to watch in any agent pitch: does it work inside your existing systems, and what does it do when it is confidently wrong?

Here is the October 2026 timeline. On 29 September, Meta launched Muse for small business — an agent that connects to QuickBooks, Shopify, Stripe and Slack, free for companies under 250 people. On 12 October, OpenAI began rolling out Dots: always-on agents, running on their own cloud computers with persistent memory. On 14 October, Google shipped Gemini 4 Argon globally, with a task mode that keeps working after you close the app. Anthropic, meanwhile, has been shipping computer-use agents — software that sees a screen and clicks it — since early 2025.

Four of the largest companies on earth, one month, the same word. And the word means something different in every press release. If you are trying to decide what any of this has to do with your business, the marketing will not help you. This is the honest translation.

The word that lost its meaning

“Agent” entered the vocabulary from a branch of AI research where it had a specific meaning: software that perceives its environment and takes actions to achieve a goal. A thermostat is an agent. A chess engine is an agent. Neither would be described as one in a press release in 2026, because the word has been repurposed to mean something closer to “a chatbot with tools.”

The practical consequence: when a vendor says “agent” in 2026, they might mean a text box that calls a weather API, or they might mean software with its own email account that negotiates with your suppliers. Both are called agents. The difference between them is the difference between a search engine and an employee, and the marketing deliberately blurs it.

What actually makes an agent

Strip the branding away and four properties separate a real agent from an assistant. All four are necessary; having one or two is a demo, having all four is a colleague:

  1. It acts, not just answers. The distinguishing test is simple: does it change the state of the world outside its own window? Send the email, book the slot, update the record, chase the invoice. If its output is text you then have to act on, it is an assistant — useful, but the work has been rearranged, not removed.
  2. It holds state across steps. A real agent remembers that step three failed and retries differently. Dots gives each agent its own computer and its own memory precisely so context survives between sessions — the thing a chatbot structurally cannot do.
  3. It works inside your systems. The 2026 agents connect to your calendar, your inbox, your accounting, your CRM. Not a new destination you visit — work happening inside the tools the business already runs on.
  4. It keeps going without you. Google’s task mode is the clearest case: give it an errand, close the app, come back to the result. The work happens between conversations, which is the entire difference between an assistant and a colleague.

Notice what is not on that list: intelligence. The model powering a 2026 agent is not dramatically smarter than the model powering a 2024 chatbot. What changed is the harness — the scaffolding of tools, memory and permissions around the model — and that is an engineering achievement, not an intelligence one. Which is why the smartest model in the world is not automatically the best agent, and why an agent is only as good as what it is allowed to touch.

What they can’t do yet

The most honest measurement available is the Remote Labour Index, published in 2026 by researchers at the Center for AI Safety and Scale AI. They did not benchmark models on trivia. They hired 240 real freelance projects — across 23 categories, roughly 6,000 hours of human work, about $140,000 of real client value — and gave the projects to AI agents with real briefs, real deadlines and real payment.

The results were sobering and specific. The best agent, Manus, completed 2.5% of projects end-to-end. GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Grok 4, the ChatGPT agent and Gemini 2.5 Pro all landed between 0.8% and 2.1%. The average project paid around $633 for about 29 hours of work. The failures were consistent and instructive: truncated outputs, corrupted files, floor plans that did not match their briefs — and, above all, failure on the client-facing parts, the iteration on feedback, the taste, the accountability to a person expecting a result.

Read those failure modes carefully. The agents did not fail at the structured, repetitive, multi-system work — the gathering, the scheduling, the following up. They failed at the judgment work: understanding what the client actually wanted, revising in response to criticism, delivering something a human would accept without checking. The 2.5% is a measurement of how far agents are from replacing people-shaped work. It is not a measurement of how far they are from carrying volume-shaped work, which they already do.

The question was never “can an agent do my job?” The answer to that is no, and will be no for longer than anyone admits. The question is “which parts of my team’s week are volume-shaped, and how fast can a machine take those?” That question has an answer, and it is usually surprising.

The line that decides everything

The single design decision that determines whether an agent is a colleague or a liability is where it stops. Not what it can do — what it must not do without asking. Every deployment needs one line, drawn per workflow: below it, the software acts on its own; above it, a person decides. Agree the price reduction? Above the line. Issue the refund? Above the line. Send the routine confirmation? Below.

This is not caution for its own sake. It is the economics. An agent that is right 95% of the time and occasionally promises a refund outside policy has not saved you 95% of the work — it has created a new job, checking it, and a new risk, when nobody is checking. The MIT NANDA report found the same thing from the enterprise side: the pilots that failed were usually not badly built, they were built without clear ownership, feedback loops or boundaries.

The other half of the design is recovery. What happens when the agent is confidently wrong? The answer must be built in advance: logging of every action, an off-switch that works in one action, a clear audit trail of what was done and why. An agent without an undo is a demo. An agent with an undo, a line and a log is infrastructure.

Common questions

What is the difference between an agent and a chatbot?

A chatbot answers questions in a window. An agent takes actions in other systems, holds state across steps, and continues without you. The 2026 releases from Google, OpenAI and Meta are agents by that definition; most earlier “agent” marketing was assistants with tools.

What is the Remote Labour Index and why does 2.5% matter?

A 2026 benchmark by the Center for AI Safety and Scale AI using 240 real freelance projects. The best agent completed 2.5% end-to-end, failing mainly on client-facing judgment work — which is precisely the work that should stay with people.

Did all four labs really release agents in one month?

Meta launched Muse on 29 September 2026, OpenAI began the Dots rollout on 12 October, and Google’s Gemini 4 Argon with task mode reached global availability on 14 October. Anthropic’s computer-use capability has been available since early 2025. All four are marketed as agents.

If agents only complete 2.5% of freelance projects, what are they good for?

The same benchmark’s failure modes show agents pass at structured, repetitive, multi-system follow-through and fail at judgment work. That is the exact split that defines a digital employee’s useful work.
