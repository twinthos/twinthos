A digital employee costs £1.60 an hour. The junior it replaces costs £13.

The maths of replacing a human role with software, done honestly with October 2026 list pricing: the model costs almost nothing, the true cost of running it properly is the interesting part, and the number that changes minds is the staff-hour, not the invoice.

The short version

  • At Google’s 14 October 2026 list price ($2 per million input tokens), a month of moderate conversational workload costs roughly $25 in tokens.
  • The binding economic question is not model cost but staff-hours: a £13.20/hour junior doing 11 hours of admin a week — the UK SME average — costs the business roughly £7,500 a year in wages alone.
  • A managed digital employee at £5,000/month is only rational where it removes multiple roles’ worth of follow-through work, which in small businesses it usually does, because the same hours are duplicated across three people.
  • The hidden cost that sinks the maths is human review time. If every action needs a person checking it, the saving is relocated, not removed.
  • The honest framing: the software is cheap, the trust is expensive. Buy the trust deliberately, or the cheap part will burn the hours it saved.

Every conversation about digital employees eventually becomes a conversation about price, and it usually goes wrong in the same place: somebody quotes the model’s token cost, somebody else quotes the salary it replaces, and neither number describes the decision. So here is the full arithmetic, with real numbers, at the time of writing.

The honest maths

Start with what the software actually consumes. Google’s Gemini 4 Argon entered global availability on 14 October 2026 at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. A customer enquiry — even a long one with the customer’s history attached — runs to a few thousand input tokens and a few hundred output. The monthly cost of handling hundreds of enquiries lands in the tens of dollars.

That is the number people use to argue AI is nearly free. It is true and almost irrelevant. A month of the software’s own usage for a five-person business comes out at roughly the price of a team lunch. If the entire question were tokens, there would be no article to write.

What it costs to run

The costs that actually determine the decision are the same five that appear in every failed pilot:

Cost lineWhat it actually isRough shape
TokensModel usage, list price, October 2026Tens of dollars a month
WiringConnecting email, calendar, CRM, accounting; credentials, testing, edge casesOne-off, weeks of skilled work
ConfigurationTeaching the rules: what may be promised, what needs approval, who gets toldOne-off, ongoing refinement
ReviewStaff time checking output before it reaches customers or systemsRecurring — the dangerous one
GovernanceAccess control, logging, off-switch, proof of what happenedSmall but non-negotiable

The one-off costs are the honest reason a managed service exists: the wiring and configuration are the same whether you run it once for yourself or a hundred times for others, and doing it once, well, requires skills most small businesses do not have on staff. The recurring cost is the one that decides whether the maths works at all.

The staff-hour that matters

Here is where the numbers get real, and where the UK figures come from. The American Express SME Business Barometer (July 2026) found UK small business owners and staff spend an estimated 11 hours a week on admin and finance work. At the October 2026 National Living Wage of £13.20 an hour for a worker aged 21 or over, eleven hours a week costs:

BasisHours/weekHourly costAnnual cost
One person, NLW, UK11£13.20£7,562
One person, loaded cost (NI, pension, overhead)11≈£17.00≈£9,745
Two people sharing the load11 each£13.20£15,124

Set that against the managed service at £5,000 a month — £60,000 a year — and the naive comparison says the human is four times cheaper. That comparison is wrong in a specific, checkable way: it prices one person’s eleven hours against a system that carries the same work for the whole team. The admin is not one person’s job in a small business; it is everybody’s second job. The owner does it at 10pm. The office manager does it between other tasks. The account is chased by whoever notices first.

Run it for the whole team instead of one hire — three people each spending 11 hours a week on the same category of work, which is the normal pattern — and the staff-hour cost lands near £22,700 a year at loaded cost. The software does not care whether it is Tuesday or 3am, and it never forgets the Thursday quote. The maths changes character completely.

The hidden cost that sinks the maths

Now the part most cost comparisons skip, and the part that decides whether any of this is worth doing: review time. If every action the software takes needs a person checking it before it counts, then nothing has been removed — the work has been rearranged into a harder shape. Checking is harder than doing: you have to verify a stranger’s work against your own memory.

This is why the Authority Line is not a safety disclaimer — it is the economics. Work below the line runs without review, because the failure mode is harmless: a reminder sent, a record updated, a question answered from policy. Work above the line goes to a person, because the failure mode costs money or a relationship. The design goal is to make the majority of volume below the line, so the review time is small, bounded and honest.

And then the final number, the one that matters: staff-hours returned. Not messages handled, not tokens processed, not dashboards rendered. Hours a person gets back to do the work only a person can do — the customer conversation, the judgment call, the thing they were actually hired for. If the maths does not produce that number, it is not a saving, it is an expense with better branding.
