What a digital employee really costs over three years

The quote shows a monthly number. Your business pays for setup, supervision, rework and the ability to leave too. Here's how to compare the real three-year bill.

Two providers can offer the same monthly price and leave you with very different costs. The difference usually lives in the work that wasn't included in the quote.

The short answer

A digital employee has five cost lines: defining the job, running it, the person who approves its work, the first failure, and the exit. Most quotes show one of them. Judge the three-year total against the salary the job replaces, then check what you still own at the end.

The fee is the smallest line in the bill

A monthly fee tells you what the thing costs to run. It does not tell you whether the job was written down properly, or who is answerable when it goes wrong. It does not tell you how much of a person's day the approvals will eat, or whether you can leave. Those four decide whether it survives year two, and none of them appear on the invoice.

Two quotes at the same monthly figure can differ by a factor of three over three years.

So judge it against the work as it is paid for today. Take one UK administrator on £28,000. Add 25% for employer National Insurance, pension and the overhead of a desk and laptop, and the real cost is about £35,000 a year. That is £2,917 a month, or £17.95 an hour across 1,950 productive hours. Compare three years of that with three years of a licence.

A quote that only shows the run line looks cheaper than it is. A quote that shows all five is the one to take seriously, even if the monthly figure is higher.

Five lines make the three-year total

Every implementation has the same five costs. Definition, run, oversight, failure, exit. Most quotes show you the second one and stop.

Read the table as five questions rather than five prices. Can they show you the job written down? Is the fee all in? How many approvals a day? What happens the first time it is wrong? What do you keep if you leave?

A supplier who can only produce one of the five has not priced the work. Ask for the rest in writing.

The setup fee is noise. The approver is not.

The figures below are example. They show the method, not a price. Run it with your own numbers in the same five rows and the picture is obvious.

Four administrators at £29,375 loaded cost £117,500 a year, which is £352,500 over three years. The digital employee: £3,000 to define the job once, and £60,000 a year to run. Add half an hour a day of oversight at £6,000 a year, and one failure budgeted at £5,000. Three-year total £206,000. You keep £146,500.

The definition fee is noise. £3,000 against £206,000 is 1.5%, which is why arguing about setup cost is a distraction. Oversight is not noise. At two hours a day the case weakens fast, and that is a design problem rather than a pricing one.

Half an hour a day is £6,000 a year of someone's time

Anything consequential needs a person to sign it off. That is the reason work can be delegated at all, and the cost belongs to you.

So design the job so approvals are batched and rare. Forty small decisions a day has not removed work. It has moved someone from doing to checking, which is often worse. One exception report at 4pm with three items on it has genuinely given time back. In a demo, count the approvals, not the tasks.

An employee who escalates is worth more than one that guesses well. The point is to make the queue short enough that you actually read it.

Budget for the first failure, and for leaving

Every first implementation has a failure. Cheap and visible means a rule catches it, it stops, it escalates, and the log shows which record was wrong. Expensive and silent means it ran for three weeks and nobody noticed. So run a short pilot on your own documents, with the error rate measured before you commit. That turns an unknown risk into a number you can accept or reject.

Then ask what you keep. The job definition, the company memory and the audit log should be yours. If any of them lives only in a supplier's console, the monthly fee is rent on your own operating knowledge. The model underneath should be swappable fuel.

What this means for you

Ask for all five lines in writing before you compare anything. Multiply the daily approvals by the approver's hourly cost. A quote with a higher monthly fee and every line shown is the one to take seriously.

Questions people actually ask

What does a digital employee cost to set up?

Setup is the cost of writing the job down properly: what goes in, what comes out, what it must never do, and who it escalates to. That work is the product, and a supplier who charges nothing for it has usually skipped the definition.

Is the person approving the work a real cost?

Yes, and it is the one buyers forget. Half an hour a day is about £6,000 a year of someone's attention. Treat a longer approval queue as a design fault, not a staffing problem.

Can we move to another supplier?

Only if you can take the memory with you. Ask what format it is exported in and whether the job definition is a document you own, because a supplier who cannot answer that is charging rent.
