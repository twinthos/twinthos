The AI that answers your phone now costs $2 a month. Almost nobody has switched.

On 14 October 2026 Google shipped its long-running agent model at $2 per million input tokens. A week earlier OpenAI started rolling out always-on agents. The economics of a digital employee have collapsed — but the reasons businesses have not moved have nothing to do with price.

The short version

  • Google’s Gemini 4 Argon entered global availability on 14 October 2026 at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, with a task mode that keeps working after you close the app.
  • OpenAI’s Dots (12 October) ships always-on agents on their own cloud computers, with persistent memory, to ChatGPT Pro and Business Premium subscribers first.
  • The token cost of a conversational agent is now measured in single-digit dollars per month. The cost of running one properly — wiring, reviewing, correcting, governing — is measured in staff-hours.
  • The binding constraint on digital employees is not model quality or price. It is trust: which actions can the software take without asking, and what happens when it is confidently wrong.
  • The model race is a commodity race now. The durable work is the operating layer around the model — the part that connects to your inbox, calendar and accounts, and stops at the line where judgment is required.

There are weeks in technology where the ground shifts so quietly that nobody notices until later. This was one of them. On 12 October 2026 OpenAI began rolling out Dots — always-on agents running on their own cloud computers, with persistent memory, that pursue goals between conversations. On 14 October, Google’s Gemini 4 Argon entered global availability with pricing that would have been implausible eighteen months ago: $2 per million input tokens, $10 per million output. Meta had already put its small business agent, Muse, in front of companies under 250 people.

Put the pieces together and the message is unmistakable: the big labs have moved from selling intelligence to selling labour. Not answers to questions — workers that hold state, take actions in other apps, and keep going after you close the tab. The question for a business owner is no longer whether the technology works. It is what to do with it.

The week the floor fell out

Google’s task mode is the clearest signal of the shift. You give it an errand — book something, research something, fill in something — and it keeps working after you leave, reporting back later. It connects to 40-plus apps including Instacart, OpenTable, Adobe and Shopify. OpenAI’s Dots go further in one respect: each agent gets its own computer and its own memory, so it can accumulate context about your business the way a colleague would.

The interesting part is the pricing logic. At $2 per million input tokens, a single customer conversation — even a long one, with context attached — costs fractions of a cent. A month of everyday enquiries, the ones your team answers on autopilot, comes out in low single-digit dollars. The model is no longer the expensive part of a digital employee. It is arguably the cheapest part.

What $2 actually buys

A worked example, using public list pricing at the time of writing. One customer enquiry, resolved end-to-end by an agent: the conversation, the lookup against your records, the confirmation, the note afterwards. Round the token usage generously — say 20,000 input and 3,000 output tokens for a long, messy exchange — and the arithmetic is uncomfortable for anyone selling expensive software:

Twenty-five dollars a month for a month of enquiry handling — at list price, before any discounts, before any batching. And that assumes every enquiry is a long one. This is why the honest answer to “why does a managed digital employee cost thousands a month” is not the tokens. The tokens are nearly free now. It is everything around them.

The real cost is not tokens

The expensive part of a digital employee is the work that makes it trustworthy, and most of it has not changed since the models were expensive:

  • Wiring it to reality. The model does not know your inbox, your calendar, your accounting system or which of your 400 customers is on a payment plan. Somebody connects it, and that somebody needs access, credentials and care.
  • Teaching it your rules. What counts as a valid refund? Which quotes need approval? Who do we notify when a VIP complains? This is institutional knowledge that lives in people’s heads, not in documentation.
  • Reviewing it honestly. An agent that is right 95% of the time is a liability if the 5% is expensive — and the 5% is always the part you cannot predict.
  • Drawing the line. Every workflow needs a defined boundary: below it the software acts, above it a person decides. Getting this wrong in either direction — too permissive or too cautious — wastes the entire investment.
  • Governing it. Access control, logging, the ability to prove what the system did and why, and the ability to switch it off in one action when something goes wrong.

None of that work scales with token prices. When the model gets ten times cheaper, the wiring, teaching and governance cost the same as before — which means they become the majority of the total. The commodity part collapsed; the craft part did not.

There is a second reason businesses have not switched, and it is more interesting than cost. The MIT NANDA report in August 2025 found roughly 95% of generative AI pilots failing to reach measurable profit-and-loss impact — and found the biggest obstacles were not model quality but integration, data readiness and the workflow itself. Price was never the barrier. Integration and trust were.

What the models still can’t do

Honesty about the limits. The Remote Labour Index — a 2026 benchmark of 240 real freelance projects published by researchers at the Center for AI Safety and Scale AI — found the best agent completing just 2.5% of projects end-to-end, with major models scoring between 0.8% and 2.1%. Agents were given real client briefs, real deadlines and real payment. They failed at the long tail: client communication, iteration on feedback, delivering something a human would accept.

That number is usually quoted as a takedown. Read it the other way: the work agents fail at is the judgment work — the part with a client on the other end expecting taste, tact and accountability. The work they pass at, in the same benchmark and in every production deployment so far, is the structured, repetitive, multi-system follow-through that consumes human afternoons. The lesson is not that agents are useless. It is that they should carry the second kind of work and leave the first kind with people.

Which is the whole design brief, really. A digital employee should be allowed to do the work a person does not want to do, and forbidden — not politely discouraged, but technically forbidden — from doing the work that needs a person’s judgment on it. The Authority Line, drawn per workflow, is the mechanism. The models are ready. The discipline is the product.

Common questions

Is Gemini 4 Argon really $2 per million input tokens?

That was the introductory global pricing announced 14 October 2026, alongside $10 per million output tokens. List prices at AI labs change frequently; the figure is correct at the time of writing and cited from the launch coverage.

What is the difference between a chatbot and an agent?

A chatbot answers. An agent acts — it uses tools, holds state across a task, and completes multi-step work. The 2026 releases from Google, OpenAI and Meta are agents: they operate inside other apps and continue after you close the conversation.

If tokens are nearly free, why is a managed digital employee still expensive?

The expense is integration, configuration, review and governance — the staff work that makes a model useful inside a specific business. Token cost is now a rounding error in the total.

What does the 2.5% Remote Labour Index result mean for businesses?

It measures agents completing full freelance projects end-to-end, including client-facing judgment work. The low score says agents are not ready to replace client-facing roles wholesale — not that they cannot carry structured follow-through, which they already do reliably.
