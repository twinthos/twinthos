What the public AI benchmarks actually measure, and what they don't

MMLU, GPQA, SWE-bench, AIME, LMArena. Every lab cites them. Each one has a narrow test set, a known failure mode, and a year-old headline number. A reading guide.

By Twinthos ResearchPublished 14 October 202610 sources, all linked

  2. The major ones, and what they actually test
  3. What the numbers do not tell you

The short version

  • A benchmark is a fixed set of questions with a fixed scoring rule. The score is useful only as long as the questions are real and the scoring is honest.
  • The five benchmarks cited most often in 2025 and 2026 are MMLU-Pro, GPQA, SWE-bench Verified, AIME 2024, and Humanity's Last Exam.
  • Each has a known failure mode. MMLU is multiple choice and can be gamed. GPQA is hard but narrow. SWE-bench is real GitHub work but only 500 issues. AIME is contest maths. HLE has been overtaken by frontier models.
  • Public leaderboards (LMArena, Artificial Analysis, Stanford HELM) try to mitigate this with blind tests and a moving target. They are useful, but the leaderboard rank is not the same as the model's business value.
  • Use benchmarks as a way to spot a model that is clearly worse on your shape of problem, not as a way to pick a winner.

Every major lab release in 2025 and 2026 was accompanied by a benchmark table. The model is faster, the model is cheaper, the model is the best in class on SWE-bench Verified or state of the art on GPQA Diamond. The numbers are usually right. They are rarely enough to make a decision.

Why benchmarks exist

A benchmark is a fixed set of questions and a fixed scoring rule. Two models face the same questions, the same inputs, the same judge. The result is a number, and the number is comparable across labs and across time. That comparability is the point. Without it, every lab has a slide deck and a different definition of better.

The catch is the same as the strength. A benchmark only measures what it sets out to measure. If the test is a thousand multiple-choice questions, the model that is best at multiple choice wins, regardless of how it does on your real task. If the test is leaked, the score is meaningless. If the test is the same for two years, the model can overfit to it.

The major ones, and what they actually test

Five benchmarks turn up in most launch posts. Here is what each is, in plain English, with the limit.

  • MMLU-Pro. 12,000 multiple-choice questions across academic subjects, released by TIGER-Lab in 2024 as a harder version of MMLU. A model with high MMLU-Pro knows a lot of facts. It does not mean the model can do your job. The original MMLU is essentially saturated; MMLU-Pro is the version with a moving target.[1]
  • GPQA Diamond. 198 graduate-level science questions that the authors say are Google-proof; a PhD holder who is not a specialist in the question's subject still cannot easily look them up. The questions are real, narrow and hard. A model that scores well on GPQA knows graduate-level science. It does not mean the model can write a safe SQL join.[2]
  • SWE-bench Verified. 500 real software engineering issues, drawn from twelve popular open-source Python repositories, with the official fix, test, and a human review of every issue. A model that scores well on SWE-bench Verified can finish real GitHub issues. The score is a percentage, not a statement about your codebase, which is most likely not one of the twelve.[3]
  • AIME 2024. The 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination, 15 integer-answer questions. A model that scores well on AIME can solve competition maths. The result is an integer in a range. A model that misses by one is scored the same as a model that misses by a million. The benchmark is good at what it tests; it is not a proxy for reasoning.[4]
  • Humanity's Last Exam (HLE). 2,500 questions across more than a hundred subjects, written by subject-matter experts and released in January 2025. The point was to build a benchmark that frontier models could not yet top. Top models passed 40% within a year. Use HLE to see if a model has clear ceiling in obscure domains; do not use it as a tie-breaker between two close models.[5]

What the numbers do not tell you

Three things that show up in business work and do not show up in benchmarks.

  • Cost per task. A model that scores 80% on SWE-bench at $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens (Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.5 list pricing) is a different business from a model that scores 78% at $0.10 / $0.40 (Google Gemini 2.0 Flash list pricing). The benchmark does not know what you pay.[6][7]
  • Latency. A model that takes 12 seconds to answer a question is unusable in a chat window. Most public benchmarks do not measure time-to-first-token.
  • Reliability on your data. A model that scores 85% on MMLU-Pro can still mis-spell your customer's name. The benchmark does not know your prompt format, your tools, or your data.

How the leaderboards try to stay fair

Public leaderboards try to fix some of this. LMArena (formerly LMSYS Chatbot Arena) puts two anonymous models side by side and asks real users which answer they prefer; the rankings are blind and the votes are public.[8] Stanford HELM publishes results across many benchmarks at once, with a fixed set of prompts and a fixed cost model.[9] Artificial Analysis publishes independent measurements of speed, cost and quality.[10] All three have known limits: LMArena can reward verbosity, HELM freezes its test set so models catch up, Artificial Analysis measures against the public APIs, not the deployment your team uses.

A reading routine for any new model claim

When a new model is released, run a small routine.

  1. What was the test? Find the benchmark name. MMLU-Pro is real but multiple choice. SWE-bench Verified is real GitHub work. A press-release chart that just says 70% on our internal eval is not a benchmark.
  2. What is the cost? List price per million input and output tokens. A 5% quality gain at 3x the cost is not a clear win for a chatty workload.
  3. What is the latency? Time to first token, time to finish a 500-token answer. Check the leaderboard or run your own.
  4. What does it fail on? Every release has a list. The list is more useful than the headline number.
  5. How does it do on your data? Run 50 real prompts. Grade them yourself. The benchmark does not know your work. You do.

Benchmarks are the most useful when they tell you a model is clearly worse on your shape of problem. They are the most misleading when they tell you one model is 3% better than another. A 3% gap is below the noise floor of any benchmark and below the noise floor of your own evaluation. Pick the model on cost, latency, reliability and taste, not on a leaderboard rank.

Common questions

Which AI benchmark should I trust most?

There is no single best benchmark. SWE-bench Verified is the most useful for software work. MMLU-Pro is the most useful for general knowledge. GPQA Diamond is the most useful for graduate-level science. Read what each is testing before reading the number.

Are benchmark scores reliable?

The scores are usually correct, on the benchmark's test set. They are not reliable as a measure of real-world performance for your work. The model that wins a benchmark is the model that was trained hardest on the benchmark's test distribution.

Why do model launches always show benchmark charts?

Because the chart is a tidy, comparable number. The cost of each call, the latency of each call, the model's failure modes on your data and the model's behaviour under a malicious prompt are not tidy. The chart is the easy part to publish, and so the chart is what gets published.

Has any benchmark not been gamed?

Public benchmarks are gamed whenever they become high-stakes. A standard mitigation is to keep the test set private and only reveal a held-out portion (SWE-bench Verified does this). A more durable mitigation is to keep moving the test set so that no model can overfit.
