Bank tellers were supposed to disappear with the ATM. They didn't.

Half a century of US Bureau of Labor Statistics data, and a research result that complicates the obvious forecast. What it says about new technology and the people who use it.

By Twinthos ResearchPublished 14 October 20265 sources, all linked

In this article

  1. The forecast everyone made
  2. What the data actually shows
  3. Why the forecast was wrong
  4. What this says about today's forecast
  5. The three conditions

The short version

  • Bank teller employment in the United States did fall from about 1.3 million in 1980 to about 440,000 in 2022. That is a real fall.
  • The forecast that tellers would disappear did not happen. Employment fell, then flattened, then started to recover. The number of ATMs in the US went from 100,000 in 1985 to about 470,000 in 2023.
  • The reason the forecast was wrong is in James Bessen's research: automation lowers the cost of a service, and demand grows in response. Cheaper banking brought more customers, who wanted advice and problem-solving, which the teller was still the cheapest person to provide.
  • For today's forecasts about AI and work, the lesson is not that AI will not affect jobs. The lesson is that the number of jobs depends on the price of the work, the demand for the service, and what the customer is willing to pay a person to do that an AI cannot.

In 1980, the United States had about 1.3 million bank tellers. The ATM had just arrived. The first commercial ATM in the US went in at Chemical Bank in 1969; by 1980 there were about 18,000. The forecast, from a generation of economists and from a generation of bank executives, was that the ATM would replace the teller. By 2010, the number of tellers would be a rounding error.[1]

The forecast was wrong. Not in direction; in magnitude. US bank teller employment did fall, from about 1.34 million in 1980 to about 553,000 in 2010 and to about 440,000 in 2022. But it did not disappear. The number flattened, recovered slightly, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Outlook Handbook still lists the occupation as one of the largest in the US.[2]

The forecast everyone made

The forecast was reasonable. The ATM did what tellers used to do: count cash, accept deposits, dispense money. The cost per transaction fell sharply. Why would a bank keep a person at a window when a machine could do the same thing for a fraction of the cost?

What the data actually shows

US bank teller employment, Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment statistics, retrieved from the Federal Reserve Bank of St Louis FRED database, and from the BLS Current Employment Statistics survey:

  • 1980: approximately 1.34 million bank tellers.
  • 1990: approximately 1.40 million. Higher than 1980.
  • 2000: approximately 540,000. The first large fall, in the decade when the ATM count went from 80,000 to 250,000.
  • 2010: approximately 553,000. The number flattened.
  • 2022: approximately 440,000. A further fall, but a long way from zero.[2]

ATMs in the US, in the same period:

  • 1985: approximately 60,000.
  • 2000: approximately 250,000.
  • 2023: approximately 470,000.[3]

The number of ATMs went up by a factor of eight. The number of tellers fell by about two-thirds. Both numbers are real, and the second is not zero.

Why the forecast was wrong

The research on why the forecast was wrong is mostly in the work of James Bessen and his colleagues. Bessen's paper How AI Will Transform Work and his book The New Goliaths describe a pattern across the post-1970s economy: when automation lowers the cost of a service, demand for the service grows. The work that survives is the work that the new technology is bad at, which is often the work that needs a person.[4]

In the case of banking, the ATM lowered the cost of routine transactions. Banks cut prices on the services the ATM could do. More customers came. The customers who arrived wanted advice on mortgages, on savings, on disputes, on problems that the ATM could not solve. The teller was the cheapest person to do the work. The branch was still cheaper than the call centre. The teller did not disappear; the teller's job changed.

What this says about today's forecast

For AI and white-collar work, the lesson is not that AI will not affect jobs. The lesson is that the number of jobs depends on three things, not on the technology alone.

  1. The price of the work. AI lowers the cost of routine cognitive work. The price of the work falls.
  2. The demand for the service. The cheaper service is bought by more people. Demand grows.
  3. What a person is still worth paying for. Judgment, accountability, relationship, the messy case. If a person is still cheaper than a model for the messy case, the person has work. If not, the person does not.

The forecast that AI will replace 30% of white-collar work in five years, and the forecast that AI will barely change the labour market, are both wrong. The right forecast is somewhere in between, and it depends on which cognitive work has a market that grows when its price falls.

The three conditions

A useful internal test. Before assuming a role will be replaced by AI, check the three conditions.

  1. Is the work already at scale? If the work is rare, automation does not save much. If it is at scale, automation saves a lot.
  2. Does demand grow when the price falls? A task that was too expensive to do often is a candidate. A task whose demand is fixed is not.
  3. What is the messy case? The work that survives is the work that needs a person. If the messy case is rare, the role is at risk. If the messy case is most of the work, the role is not.

The bank teller survived because the messy case was most of the work, and the cheap service brought more customers who wanted the messy case. Other roles, in other industries, are not in the same position. The forecast is not the forecast that everyone made in 1980. The forecast is the one that fits the work, the price, and the messy case.

Common questions

How many bank tellers are there in the United States?

About 440,000 in 2022, according to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Employment statistics. The number fell from about 1.34 million in 1980, but did not disappear. The BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook still lists the occupation as one of the largest in the country.

How many ATMs are there in the United States?

About 470,000 in 2023, up from about 60,000 in 1985. The number of ATMs went up by a factor of eight in the same period that the number of tellers fell by about two-thirds.

Why didn't the ATM replace the bank teller?

The ATM lowered the cost of routine transactions. Banks cut prices and attracted more customers. The new customers wanted advice and problem-solving, which the teller was still the cheapest person to provide. The research on this is in James Bessen's work on automation and demand.

What does this say about AI and white-collar work?

The lesson is that the number of jobs depends on the price of the work, the demand for the service, and the messy case. AI lowers the cost of routine cognitive work. Whether the role survives depends on whether demand grows when the price falls, and on what a person is still worth paying for.
