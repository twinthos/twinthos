The last time a machine was supposed to erase a job. It hired more people instead.

Everyone knows ATMs replaced bank tellers. The data says the opposite: teller employment rose for two decades after the ATMs arrived, because the machine cut the cost of a branch and banks opened more of them. There is a live lesson for anyone reading this week’s AI headlines.

The short version

  • US bank teller employment did not collapse when ATMs spread — it grew steadily from the 1980s into the 2010s, peaking near 600,000 around 2010 before digital banking changed the picture.
  • Economist James Bessen’s research explains why: the ATM cut the cost of operating a branch, banks opened more branches, and demand for tellers rose with them. Automation raised the volume of work, not just the speed of it.
  • The same mechanism ran earlier with the telephone exchange: each operator could handle far more calls, so companies staffed more operators, not fewer, until the network effect reversed it.
  • The mechanism is not “automation creates jobs” in the abstract. It is that a cheaper input makes the service it powers cheaper, which makes customers buy more of it.
  • Read this week’s AI jobs warnings with that in mind: exposure is not extinction, and the history says the job changes shape before it changes count.

Every wave of automation produces one story that becomes shorthand for all the others, and for the last forty years that story has been the bank teller. The narrative is tidy: the ATM arrives, the teller disappears, a whole job category becomes a museum exhibit. It appears in every discussion of AI and jobs, usually as the cautionary tale that proves this time really is different.

The data does not agree. In fact, for roughly two decades after ATMs spread across America, bank teller employment grew — steadily, through the entire period when the machines were supposedly eating the job. This is not a contrarian reading of the evidence. It is the evidence, and the research explaining it is now standard in economics.

The story everyone knows

The intuitive version is so clean it almost has to be true. A teller spends the day counting cash, processing deposits, answering questions about balances. A machine arrives that does the counting and the dispensing. The branch needs fewer people. Extrapolate across 200,000 branches and the job should vanish.

The error in that version is treating the teller’s job as fixed. In reality the branch was a bundle of work: the cash handling plus the relationships, the judgement calls, the sales conversations, the local knowledge. The machine took one item from the bundle. What the bank did with the freed capacity depended on what else it could do with a branch — and what it did was open more of them.

What the data says

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics has tracked bank teller employment for decades. The series is unambiguous: teller employment rose through the 1980s, 1990s and 2000s — precisely the years ATMs went from novelty to furniture — and only began a sustained decline around 2010, when online and mobile banking, not ATMs, finally changed where customers did their banking. The Federal Reserve’s own data on the number of ATMs shows the machine count and the teller count climbing together for most of that period.

The research that explains this is Boston University economist James Bessen’s work on automation and demand. His finding, later popularised in Learning by Doing: when a technology makes a service cheaper to provide, demand for the service expands, and the labour needed to serve the expanded demand can exceed what the technology saved. The ATM cut the operating cost of a branch by roughly a quarter. Branches became cheaper to run. Banks opened more branches. More branches needed more tellers — now doing less cash counting and more of the work that branches existed for.

The same pattern shows up in the telephone exchanges of the early twentieth century. Each automatic switchboard let a single operator handle many more calls. The prediction was mass unemployment among operators. What happened, as Bessen documents, is that the cost of phone service fell, subscriptions rose, and operator employment grew with them — until the technology matured enough to eliminate the role entirely, decades later than anyone predicted.

Automation rarely erases a job on the day it arrives. It changes what the job is, changes the economics of the business around it, and only then, sometimes, changes the count.

Why the machine created work

The mechanism behind the ATM story is not mysterious, and it is not “technology creates jobs” in some abstract benevolent sense. It is arithmetic. A cheaper input lowers the cost of the output it feeds into. Customers respond to cheaper output by buying more. The expanded volume needs servicing, and the servicing needs people — even if each unit of output needs fewer of them than before.

The teller’s day is the cleanest illustration. In 1980, maybe half a teller’s hours went on transactions the machine now handles. By 1995 those hours were free — free for the cross-selling, the problem-solving, the customer relationships that were always part of the job but always squeezed out by the counting. The job did not shrink. It moved up a tier, and the business that employed the teller grew because its product got cheaper.

The catch, and the reason this is not a cheerful story, is the lag. For a generation, the job looked doomed while the employment data quietly climbed. Every newspaper column in 1985 about the disappearing teller was, in the medium run, wrong — and the people making the predictions were not being foolish. They were extrapolating from a mechanism that was real (the machine does the work) while missing a second mechanism that was also real (cheaper work means more work).

The live lesson for this week’s headlines

This connects directly to what the news said this week. On 12 October 2026 the IMF’s Kristalina Georgieva warned of a “slow-moving tsunami”, with about 40% of jobs worldwide exposed and 60% in advanced economies. On 7 October, Bluevine reported 53% of surveyed small business owners say AI has already absorbed at least one role’s workload, and 75% plan to absorb more instead of hiring.

Both stories are being read the way the 1985 columns read the ATM. The honest response to both is the one the teller data teaches: exposure is not extinction, and the second-order effect — the cheaper service, the expanded demand, the work that moves up a tier — is where the actual outcome gets decided. Nobody in 1985 could see that the ATM was quietly building a bank that needed more humans, doing better work, for another twenty-five years.

The one place the analogy genuinely strains is speed. The ATM took decades; AI took months. The teller had a generation to retrain into the newer version of the job. The admin worker watching AI absorb their inbox this autumn does not get a generation. That is not a reason to dismiss the historical pattern — it is a reason to hurry the part the pattern says matters: moving people up the tier while the machine carries the volume, not after it has already arrived.

Common questions

Did ATMs really not reduce teller employment?

They reduced teller employment per branch, but the US Bureau of Labor Statistics series shows total teller employment rising through the 1980s, 1990s and 2000s, peaking around 2010. The sustained decline came with online and mobile banking, not with the ATM rollout.

Who is James Bessen and what did he find?

A Boston University economist whose research on technology and labour, including Learning by Doing, documents that automated equipment lowered costs, expanded demand, and in many cases increased employment in the affected industries — the ATM and telephone exchange being the canonical cases.

Does this mean AI will not eliminate jobs?

It means exposure is not extinction and second-order effects decide the outcome. Bessen’s work also shows some technologies did reduce employment; the ATM case shows why prediction from the technology alone fails. The honest position is that the outcome depends on what the freed capacity is redeployed into.

Why mention this in the week of the IMF and Bluevine reports?

Because both were being read as job-eradication forecasts, and the most famous historical case of exactly that prediction says the count and the shape of jobs move on different timescales. It is the most useful available lens for this week’s numbers.
