A chatbot answers the question. A digital employee closes the file. Five differences, and only one of them is technical.

A chatbot and a digital employee are not the same product at different prices. One has no job, no memory and no audit trail. The other is priced against the salary it absorbs. Here is the comparison, the arithmetic, and seven questions to ask before buying either.

The answer in forty words

  • A chatbot answers questions. A digital employee does one named job, inside the systems you already own.
  • The difference is not intelligence. It is memory, scope, permissions, a log, and a person who signs off.
  • That is why one is sold per seat and the other is priced against the salary it absorbs.
  • If a supplier cannot tell you which of the two they are selling, they are selling the first one.

Almost every business conversation about AI this year has collapsed two different products into one word, and the collapse is expensive. It is the reason a managing director can come out of a demo impressed and still be unable to say what they bought. So this note does the boring thing properly: it puts the two objects side by side and takes them apart.

What the two things actually are

A chatbot is an interface over a large language model. You type, it answers. Modern versions are genuinely good at drafting, summarising, rewriting, explaining a policy, or turning a page of notes into an email. Sold per seat, usually monthly, priced like software because that is what it is: a tool that a person operates.

A digital employee is a different object. It has a job, a name, a place in the workflow, a definition of what it must never do, and a human who is accountable for its output. It does not wait to be asked. It picks up work from a queue, does one repeating sequence inside the software the business already runs, records what it did, and stops when something falls outside its scope.

The distinction is not philosophical. It changes what you can delegate. You cannot delegate a job to something that has no job description, and you cannot be accountable for output from something that keeps no record.

Read that table as a list of things that have to exist before work can leave a person's desk. A tool can be excellent and still have none of them, because none of them are intelligence problems. They are design decisions about scope, records and authority.

The money, and why it is priced differently

This is where the comparison stops being academic. A tool is priced against other tools, which is why the subscription column of a software bill is full of small numbers. A job is priced against the person doing it, which is why the number is larger and why the argument is different.

Take one UK administrator on £28,000. Add 25% for employer National Insurance, pension and the overhead of a desk, a laptop and management time, and the real annual cost is about £35,000. Divide by twelve: £2,917 a month. Divide by roughly 1,950 productive hours: £17.95 an hour.

That is the comparator. Not a software licence. Not a competitor's price list. The cost of the work as it is currently being paid for.

£28,000 salary → £35,000 loaded → £2,917 a month → £17.95 an hourA monthly workflow at £750 is 26% of that hire. A full desk at £5,000 is £60,000 a year, and has to replace about 1.7 salaries or add £60,000 of revenue.

Two consequences follow, and both are uncomfortable. First, a cheap implementation that only drafts text is competing with a £20 subscription, and it will lose. Second, an expensive implementation that does not displace countable work is competing with a salary it cannot beat. The only defensible price sits between those two, and it can only be found by naming the job and counting what it costs today.

Three ways the pair get confused

The demo effect. A chatbot demo is a conversation, so it always looks impressive. A digital employee demo is a queue emptying, which looks like admin. Buyers consistently choose the more exciting demo and then wonder why nothing changed.

The wrapper problem. Some suppliers put a job-shaped wrapper around a general assistant: a prompt, a name, a friendly avatar. It answers questions about invoices. It cannot open one, because it has no access, no permission and no record. The wrapper is the tell, not the product.

The accountability gap. Ask who is responsible when the output is wrong and you will hear a version of "the model". That answer means nobody. A digital employee has a person's name against consequential actions, and a log that says exactly what happened and when.

When a chatbot is the right answer

Often, honestly. If the need is to draft, summarise, translate, explain or brainstorm, buy a seat and stop there. If the work is genuinely a tool for a person to operate, a digital employee is an expensive way to solve it.

The test is simple: can you name the repeating task, say how often it happens, and count what it costs now? If yes, it is a job and it can be delegated. If the answer is a shrug, the need is a tool, or it is nothing at all.

Seven questions to ask before buying either

  1. What exactly does it produce, and who receives it? An answer, or a file, a booking, a chased document, a closed case. Vague output means vague scope.
  2. Where does it keep what it learns about us? If the answer is "in the conversation", it has no memory, whatever the marketing says.
  3. What is it not allowed to do? A supplier who cannot list prohibitions has not thought about the job.
  4. Show me the log of one completed task, end to end. Not a dashboard. A record of what it touched, in what order, and what it changed.
  5. When it stops, who does it escalate to, and how fast? The escalation rule is the safety design. If it has none, the failure is silent.
  6. What does it cost per month, and what job does that replace? Any answer that does not name the job is a software sale dressed up.
  7. How do we prove it worked in two weeks? There should be a number, agreed in advance, measured on your own material.

Questions people actually ask

Is a digital employee just a chatbot with a better prompt?

No. A prompt changes how an assistant talks. A digital employee changes what it is allowed to do. It has a written job definition, scoped permissions, a log of every action and a named human who signs off consequential ones. Strip those four things out and you are back to a chatbot, whatever it is called.

Do we need an API or a migration?

No. The desk works through the screens a person already uses: the case management system, the broking platform, the payroll software, the insurer portal, email. That is why a first job can be live in about two hours rather than in a two-year integration project.

What happens when it gets something wrong?

The job is scoped so that mistakes are caught by a rule rather than by luck, and the pilot measures the error rate on the client's own material before anything is committed. Anything consequential stops and waits for approval. An employee that escalates is worth more than one that guesses confidently.

Which model does it run on?

The model is fuel and it is swappable. The value is the memory, the job definition, the permissions and the audit trail around the model, not the model itself.

Can we start with one job?

That is the only way it is sold. One job, one desk, measured on a countable outcome over a short pilot. If the job cannot be counted, it is not a good first job.

What we will not claim

That a digital employee replaces a team. It replaces a job, and jobs are smaller than teams. That it never makes mistakes: it will, which is why the job is scoped so a rule catches them and why the pilot measures the rate. That the model matters more than the design around it: it does not, and any supplier arguing otherwise is selling you a seat.
