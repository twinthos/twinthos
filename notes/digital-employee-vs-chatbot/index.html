Chatbot or digital employee? What you are actually buying

A chatbot answers what you ask and forgets you. A digital employee does one job, remembers your rules and keeps a record. Here is the gap, and the money.

The short answer

A chatbot answers questions and forgets you between sessions. A digital employee does one named job inside the systems you already use. It remembers your rules, logs what it did, stops when work falls outside its scope, and has a person who signs off the risky parts.

One answers the question. The other finishes the job.

A chatbot is a box you type into. It drafts, summarises, rewrites, explains a policy or turns a page of notes into an email. You buy it per seat, every month, because it is software and a person operates it.

A digital employee is a different object. It has a job, a name, a place in your process and a written list of things it must never do. It picks work up from a queue and does one repeating sequence in the software you already run. It records what it did, and stops when something is unfamiliar.

That sounds like a small gap. It is not. You cannot hand a job to something with no job description, and you cannot answer for work that leaves no record.

The gap is memory, scope, access, records and authority

Five things have to exist before work can leave a person's desk. Only one of them is technical.

Memory. It keeps what your business knows: prices, exceptions, the customer who must be handled carefully. A chatbot starts again every session.

Scope. One written job with a start and an end. A chatbot has no end, so it can never finish.

Access. It works inside your existing screens under your permissions, so it does not need your software rewritten.

Records. Every action logged, attributable, reversible. Most tools keep nothing.

Authority. A named person approves anything consequential, in writing. Without that name, nobody is answerable.

None of those five is a cleverness problem. They are decisions somebody has to make about what the thing is allowed to do.

You are comparing it with a salary, not a licence

A tool is priced against other tools, so it shows up in your software bill as a small number. A job is priced against the person doing it, so the number is larger and the argument changes. You stop asking what it costs and start asking what it replaces.

Take one UK administrator on £28,000. Add 25% for employer National Insurance, pension and the overhead of a desk and laptop. The real cost is about £35,000 a year: £2,917 a month, or £17.95 an hour across 1,950 productive hours.

A monthly workflow at £750 is 26% of that hire. A full desk at £5,000 a month is £60,000 a year, so it has to replace about 1.7 salaries or add £60,000 of revenue. A cheap tool that only drafts text loses to a £20 subscription. An expensive job that removes no countable work loses to the salary.

That is the comparator. Not a competitor's price list, but the cost of the work as you pay for it today.

Three ways buyers get fooled

The demo effect. A chatbot demo is a conversation, so it always looks impressive. A digital employee demo is a queue emptying, which looks like admin. Buyers pick the exciting demo, then wonder why nothing changed.

The wrapper problem. Some suppliers wrap a general assistant in a job-shaped shell: a prompt, a name, an avatar. It will answer questions about invoices. It cannot open one, because it has no access, no permission and no record.

The accountability gap. Ask who is responsible when the output is wrong and you will hear a version of “the model”. That answer means nobody.

Sometimes the chatbot is the right buy

Often it is. If you need drafting, summarising, translating, explaining or thinking out loud, buy a seat and stop there. A digital employee is an expensive way to solve that.

The test is one question. Can you name the repeating task, say how often it happens and count what it costs now? If yes, it is a job and it can be delegated. If the answer is a shrug, you need a tool or nothing.

Five questions to ask before you sign

What does it produce, and who receives it? An answer, or a file, a booking, a chased document, a closed case. A supplier who cannot name the output has not written the job.

Where does it keep what it learns about you? If the answer is “in the conversation”, it has no memory, whatever the marketing says.

What is it not allowed to do? A supplier who cannot list prohibitions has not thought about the job.

Show me the record of one completed task, end to end. Not a dashboard. If they show you a dashboard, ask again.

When it stops, who does it escalate to, and how fast? If there is no escalation rule, the failure is silent.

What this means for you

A chatbot is a tool you operate. A digital employee is cover for a named job, with memory, limits, a record and a human who signs off. The price follows: one is sold per seat, the other against the salary it absorbs. Before you buy either, write the job down and count what it costs you now.

Questions people actually ask

Is a digital employee just a chatbot with a better prompt?

No. A prompt changes how an assistant talks. A digital employee changes what it is allowed to do. The job is written down, permissions are limited, every action is logged, and a named person signs off the risky ones. Remove those four and you are back to a chatbot, whatever the label says.

Do we need an API or a migration?

No. It works through the screens your team already uses, from the case system to email. That is why a first job can be live in about two hours rather than in a two-year integration project.

What happens when it gets something wrong?

The job is written so a rule catches the mistake rather than luck catching it. A short pilot measures the error rate on your own material before you commit, and anything consequential stops and waits for a person to approve it.
