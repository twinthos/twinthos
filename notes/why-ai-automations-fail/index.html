95% of AI pilots fail. The number has been stable for two years.

MIT’s August 2025 State of AI in Business report found roughly 95% of generative AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact. McKinsey and BCG independently report the same wall. The pattern behind it is not technical — and it is fixable.

The short version

  • MIT NANDA’s August 2025 report found about 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots show no measurable profit-and-loss impact, with only 5% of integrated tools reaching production.
  • McKinsey’s State of AI 2024 found 65% of organisations report no EBIT impact from generative AI; BCG found 74% of companies struggle to achieve and scale value, and only 4% are future-built.
  • The failure is almost never the model. It is the workflow around it: the moment the AI’s output hits a handoff that still depends on a person remembering to push it.
  • Gartner’s 2025 prediction: at least 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, on cost, unclear value and risk.
  • The pattern that works is unglamorous: pick one workflow, wire it into the systems it touches, give it a clear authority line, and measure the staff-hours it removes.

In August 2025 the MIT Media Lab’s NANDA initiative published The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025. The finding that travelled furthest: about 95% of custom generative AI tools built by organisations — and pilots of purchased ones — produced no measurable impact on profit and loss. Only 5% of integrated AI tools reached production with any return. The report was blunt about why: the barrier was not model quality but “learning gap” — tools that do not learn from feedback and do not fit how work actually flows.

That would be one striking study. Except McKinsey’s State of AI 2024 found 65% of respondents reporting no EBIT impact from generative AI. BCG’s Where’s the Value in AI (2024) found 74% of companies struggle to achieve and scale value from AI, with just 4% qualifying as future-built — and those 4% earning above-average returns. Gartner went further, predicting that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025.

Three independent research organisations, three different methods, the same wall. When a number is that stable across that many studies, it stops being a statistic and starts being a structural fact about how businesses adopt this technology.

The number that won’t die

The reflex reading of 95% is that AI is overhyped. The actual reading, the one the reports support, is narrower and more useful: AI demos well and deploys badly. Show the model a task and it impresses. Put the model inside the company — connected to the CRM, the inbox, the calendar, the accounting system, the phone — and something breaks.

MIT’s report is specific about what breaks. The tools that succeed share traits: they are integrated into existing systems rather than bolted on as a new destination; they learn from human feedback; they are owned by line managers rather than innovation teams; and they solve a narrow, expensive, recurring workflow rather than a broad ambition like “digital transformation”. The 95% fail almost all of those tests.

The failure is almost never “the AI got the answer wrong.” It is “the AI got the answer right and nothing happened,” because the next step in the process was still a person’s job.

Where it actually breaks

Map almost any failed pilot onto the same anatomy. A marketing team fine-tunes a writing model. It produces drafts. The drafts sit in a queue. A human reviews, edits and posts them. The review step — the handoff between software and person — was always the bottleneck, and it still is. The pilot “worked”; nothing changed. Six months later the licence is cancelled and the project is filed under lessons learned.

The second failure mode is the one Bluevine’s survey documented from the other side: the pilot did change things, but it moved the work rather than removing it. The drafting got faster, so the human’s job became reviewing drafts — harder work, invisible work, unmeasured work. The pilot reports a 40% efficiency gain; the team reports burnout. Both are telling the truth about different halves of the same deployment.

The third mode is boundary failure. An agent takes an action it should not have: a refund promised outside policy, a quote sent at old prices, a message sent to the wrong customer. The response is understandable and expensive: pull the permissions, hand everything back to humans, cancel the pilot. The software did not fail. The absence of a clearly drawn authority line — the thing that tells the software where it may act and where it must stop — failed.

The software-shaped gap

There is a category of work that is nearly invisible in process documentation and dominates small business operations: the work that exists only because a system cannot talk to another system. The CRM holds the record; a human has to notice the record changed. The invoice was paid; a human has to see the payment and update the project. The booking was made in the calendar app; a human has to copy it into the schedule.

This is Operational Drag — the invisible tax on work that only moves when someone remembers to push it — and it is exactly the shape that AI fails at when deployed naively, because each individual push is trivial and the total is enormous. Nobody writes a workflow document for “remember to chase the quote on Thursday.” It is not a process. It is a person, being conscientious, every day.

Naive pilots miss it for the same reason audits miss it: it is not in any system, so it is not in any screenshot. A tool connected to the CRM can only do what the CRM records. The drag lives in the gaps between records, and those gaps are maintained by people, deliberately, because the systems cannot bridge them.

What working looks like

The pattern across the 5% that work, and across Twinthos’s own deployments, is consistent enough to write down:

  1. Pick one expensive, recurring, annoying workflow. Not “AI strategy”. “Chasing outstanding quotes.” The narrower the better; narrowness is what makes wiring tractable.
  2. Connect it to the systems the workflow already touches. Email, calendar, CRM, accounting. No new destination for staff to visit — the work happens inside the tools they use.
  3. Draw the authority line before you switch it on. Below the line: the software acts. Above the line: a person decides. Every action type gets classified once, deliberately.
  4. Measure staff-hours removed, not messages sent. The only honest metric is time returned to humans, counted the same way before and after.
  5. Keep a human in the loop where judgment lives — and measure the review cost, so the saving is real and not just relocated.

None of this requires the frontier model of the month. It requires the boring engineering of integration, the discipline of boundaries, and the honesty to count the checking as part of the cost. That is why the failure rate is stable: the technology improves every quarter, and the hard part never was the technology.

Common questions

Where does the 95% figure come from?

MIT Media Lab NANDA, The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025, published August 2025. It surveyed 300 public deployments, 52 interviews and 153 executives, and found roughly 95% of custom or pilot generative AI tools with no measurable P&L impact.

Is the 95% number about bad technology?

No. The same research found the barriers are integration, workflow fit and organisational adoption — tools that do not connect to where work happens and do not learn from feedback. The models pass the demos; the deployments fail the handoffs.

Why do McKinsey, BCG and MIT all land in the same place?

Different methods, same mechanism: value appears only when the tool is wired into the workflow and owned by the people doing the work. Pilots skip both. Independent replication of a structural problem is a signal, not a coincidence.

What does Gartner’s 30% abandonment prediction mean?

Gartner predicted at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs and unclear business value.
