AI has a new name. SI is now in US government documents — here is what actually changed.

On 29 September 2026 the US government ordered federal agencies to say super intelligence and SI instead of artificial intelligence and AI. Here is what actually happened, why experts say the new word was already taken, and why none of it changes what your business should be doing on Monday.

On 29 September 2026 the United States government changed the name of the technology everybody has been arguing about. The order directs federal agencies to use “super intelligence” and “SI” in place of “artificial intelligence” and “AI” in official communications, websites, reports and policy documents, to the extent permitted by law. Politico and the BBC both reported the signing.

Nine days later the argument moved from paperwork to enemies. None of it alters what you can do with the technology on Monday morning, and that is the point. While the argument about names has been loud, the businesses pulling ahead have not been arguing about names at all. They have been asking three quieter questions: what does this system know, what is it authorised to do, and does it finish the work.

What actually happened, in order

The sequence matters, because most coverage has collapsed it into one headline. After the 29 September order came the speech at the United Nations General Assembly in New York, where the case was made in the President’s own words: “From this point forward, all of the United States’ documents, and hopefully the world’s, will be changed to use the more accurate term super as opposed to artificial. So it’s super intelligence... welcome to the new world of super intelligence — SI.”

Then, on 8 October 2026, came the Truth Social post that did most of the spreading: “The White House considers anyone that uses the term, Artificial Intelligence, as opposed to the highly accepted new and more accurate term, Super Intelligence, THE ENEMY!”

The machinery moved after the words. The White House updated its AI website at ai.gov, removing AI references and using SI in their place. Parts of the industry followed. Elon Musk pledged to rename SpaceXAI to SpaceXSI. Microsoft chief executive Satya Nadella referred to “Super Intelligence” in a post. Salesforce renamed its AIForce platform “SIForce”. And the chief executives of Google, OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic and Nvidia signed the White House Accord on Super Intelligence.

Notice what kind of list that is: renames, pledges, posts and signatures. Every item on it can be decided in an afternoon. Not one requires a model to get better at anything. The leaderboards did not move on 29 September, and your invoice processing did not improve. What moved was the vocabulary of American federal paperwork.

The word was already taken

The objection from researchers is not that “super intelligence” sounds silly. It is that the word had a job already, and was doing it well.

“Superintelligence” has long meant a hypothetical future system that exceeds human capability across the board — not better at chess, better at everything at once. PolitiFact reported experts cautioning that the rename is misleading for exactly this reason, and noting the detail underneath it: no system has achieved that. Not one.

So October 2026 is odd. The term written into official US government documents describes something that does not exist. Meanwhile the technology that genuinely does exist — useful, uneven, wrong in confident ways — has had its name retired by decree.

A name change costs nothing and buys attention. The technology underneath did not get smarter on the day it was renamed, and the work nobody in your business wants to do is still sitting there, unchanged, waiting for someone to take it.

Language sets expectations. Call the thing in your business “super intelligence” and your team will either over-trust it or roll their eyes. Call it what it is — software that reads, drafts, routes and follows up inside certain limits — and you can have a productive conversation about where those limits should sit.

Four names, four meanings

This field produces labels faster than it produces clarity. Here is the honest map of the four terms you will meet in 2026.

TermWhat it meansStatus in October 2026
AI (artificial intelligence)The broad label for software that does things we associate with thinking: writing, recognising, predicting, decidingIn use since the 1950s; now retired from US federal documents by executive order
AGI (artificial general intelligence)A system that matches a human across most tasks, rather than excelling at oneContested and undefined; no agreed test, no agreed arrival
SuperintelligenceA hypothetical system exceeding human capability across the boardHypothetical. Not achieved by any system, per experts cited by PolitiFact
SI (super intelligence)The US federal government’s replacement term for AIMandated in federal communications, websites, reports and policy documents from 29 September 2026

Three of those four are aspirations. One describes something you can buy and measure — and it is the one whose name was just taken away.

What did not change

Here is the number worth attention, from the same autumn. McKinsey’s State of AI Global Survey, published 25 August 2026 with 1,719 respondents across 97 countries, found that 40% of organisations above $1bn in revenue report scaling AI agents — up from 27%. Smaller organisations were flat at 22%.

The large organisations moved thirteen points in a year. The smaller ones did not move. Whatever else is true, the advantage is not going to whoever has the best vocabulary.

What separates them is not intelligence and not budget alone. Someone sat down and answered the three unglamorous questions before anything switched on:

  • What does it know? A model that cannot see your order history or your credit policy is a clever stranger. Connecting it to what the business actually knows is the work.
  • What is it authorised to do? Every workflow needs a line: below it the software acts alone, above it a person decides. Agencies without that line either freeze or get burned.
  • Does it finish? Not does it answer — does the work reach a conclusion without somebody chasing it? Most AI projects fail here, quietly.

None of these questions care whether you call it AI, SI, or whatever is decided next year. They care about wiring, permission and follow-through, none of which can be improved with a better word.

Common questions

What does SI stand for?

SI stands for “super intelligence.” From 29 September 2026 an executive order directs US federal agencies to use it in place of AI in official communications, websites, reports and policy documents.

Did the US government officially rename AI to SI?

It changed how federal agencies refer to the technology. The executive order was signed on 29 September 2026 and ai.gov was updated to remove AI references in favour of SI. The direction covers government documents and communications, not private speech.

What did Trump actually say about the term change?

At the UN General Assembly in New York he said: “From this point forward, all of the United States’ documents, and hopefully the world’s, will be changed to use the more accurate term super as opposed to artificial. So it’s super intelligence... welcome to the new world of super intelligence — SI.” On 8 October 2026 he wrote on Truth Social that the White House considers anyone using the term artificial intelligence “THE ENEMY!”

Is renaming AI to SI the same as achieving superintelligence?

No. Superintelligence has long meant a hypothetical system exceeding human capability across the board. Experts cited by PolitiFact caution the rename is misleading, because no system has reached that level.

Have companies adopted the new term?

Some have. Elon Musk pledged to rename SpaceXAI to SpaceXSI, Satya Nadella referred to “Super Intelligence” in a post, Salesforce renamed AIForce to SIForce, and the chief executives of Google, OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic and Nvidia signed the White House Accord on Super Intelligence.

What should a business actually do about this?

Very little differently. What decides whether the technology pays for itself is what the system knows, what it is authorised to do, and whether it finishes the work. None of that turns on the name.
