The US renamed AI to SI. Your work is exactly the same

On 29 September 2026 US agencies were told to say super intelligence, not AI. What changed for a small business: the name, not the work or the questions.

The short answer

On 29 September 2026 a US executive order told federal agencies to say super intelligence, or SI, instead of AI. It changed a word in government paperwork. It did not change what the software can do, what it costs, or the three questions that decide whether it helps you.

What actually happened, in plain order

The order was signed on 29 September 2026 and reported by Politico and the BBC. It directs US federal agencies to say "super intelligence" and "SI" instead of "artificial intelligence" and "AI". That covers official communications, websites, reports and policy files, as far as the law allows. It followed a speech at the UN General Assembly in New York, where the same change was urged on the world. The government's own AI website was then updated to drop the old term.

On 8 October 2026 a Truth Social post did most of the spreading. It said the White House considers anyone using "artificial intelligence" rather than "super intelligence" to be "THE ENEMY!" Companies moved quickly. Elon Musk pledged to rename SpaceXAI to SpaceXSI. Salesforce renamed AIForce to SIForce. Satya Nadella used the new term in a post, and the heads of Google, OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic and Nvidia signed the White House Accord on Super Intelligence.

The name is bigger than the thing

Superintelligence already had a meaning, and it was not this one. It described a hypothetical system better than humans at everything at once, not better at one job. Experts quoted by PolitiFact say no system has reached that level.

So the official name describes something that does not exist yet, while the ordinary, useful, sometimes confidently wrong software you can buy today has been renamed by decree. That is worth saying plainly, because overselling is how trust gets spent.

But read the criticism carefully. Nobody is arguing that the technology does not work. The complaint is that the new name flatters it. Two years ago the argument was whether any of this would amount to anything. That argument is over.

The number that matters more than the name

McKinsey published its State of AI survey on 25 August 2026, with 1,719 respondents across 97 countries. It found 40% of firms above $1bn in revenue are scaling AI agents, up from 27% a year earlier. Smaller firms stayed flat at 22%.

Thirteen percentage points in a year is not hype. It is thousands of real tasks handed over, measured and kept. It is also a widening gap, and the gap is made of ordinary things rather than secrets. Everyone can buy the same models. What separates the two groups is whether the work was wired up properly before anything was switched on.

Note what the 22% means. Smaller firms are not held back by the price of a model, because the same models are on sale to everyone. They are held back by the wiring and the permission, which is slower and duller work.

Three questions, none of which mention the name

First, what does it know? A system that cannot see your order history, your prices or your client emails is a clever stranger. Connecting it to what your business actually knows is the whole job, and it is the part most projects skip.

Second, what is it allowed to do? Take refunds. Below a limit you set, it decides alone. Above that, a person decides, and the request arrives with the order details attached. Draw that line for each task, in writing, and name who owns it.

Third, does it finish? Not does it answer. Does the unpaid invoice get chased until it is paid, without anyone remembering to chase it? This is where most attempts die quietly, in month two, and nobody notices because the demo looked fine.

None of those three questions care what the technology is called this year. They are about wiring, permission and follow-through, and you can answer all three this month in a business of any size.

What this means for you

Ignore the name and count the hours. Take the three tasks your team repeats most, and work out how long each one takes from start to finish, including the chasing. If the total is small, do nothing. If it is a day a week, you have a case for change, and it will not be decided by a press release.

If you cannot name those three tasks from memory, ask your team what they spent yesterday afternoon chasing.

What this means for you

A government changed its vocabulary. That tells you the technology is here to stay, and it tells you nothing about whether your business is using it well. The firms pulling ahead are not winning on terminology. They answered three plain questions: what the system knows, what it may decide alone, and whether the work actually finishes.

Questions people actually ask

What does SI stand for?

Super intelligence. From 29 September 2026 a US executive order directs federal agencies to use it instead of AI in official communications, websites, reports and policy documents.

Did that rename AI for everyone?

No. The order covers how US federal agencies write and publish, not how private companies or individuals speak. Several technology firms chose to follow, which was their decision rather than a legal requirement.

Does the new name mean superintelligence exists now?

It does not. Superintelligence has long meant a hypothetical system better than humans at everything, and experts quoted by PolitiFact say no system has reached that. The useful software you can buy today is real, uneven and worth measuring.
