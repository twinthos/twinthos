Why the same customer gets two different answers

Dell, SAP, Google, Meta and Workday all announced company memory in eight days. Your staff still spend 2.3 hours a week retyping what the business knows.

The short answer

Because the answer lives in someone's memory rather than in a record, and memories drift. A company brain is a written, approved store of what your business knows. Without one, staff spend 2.3 hours a week retyping context. Five of the biggest firms made the same bet in eight days.

One customer, two answers, one of them wrong

A customer rings on Monday to ask if you can deliver before Friday. The person who answers says yes, because that is what they were told last month. The same customer rings again on Thursday, gets someone else, and hears that Friday is not possible. Both staff were being helpful. One of them was working from a fact that had stopped being true.

That is not a training problem. The cut-off changed, the change was mentioned in a meeting, and it never reached the second person. So the answer came from memory instead of from a record. Count how often that happens in your business. That gap is what company memory means, and it is the thing five of the world's largest software firms spent the second week of October 2026 building.

Five software giants built memory for themselves in eight days

Between 6 and 13 October 2026, Dell, SAP, Google, Meta and Workday each announced something whose only job is to remember what a business already knows. None used the same word. Dell called it trusted context. Google shipped four kinds of memory. Meta shipped ActiveMemory.

Two of the five are promises rather than products. Dell's components are due in the first half of 2027. SAP's is an agreement to acquire TechWolf, expected to close in the fourth quarter of 2026. So three shipped, two are commitments, and the clustering in one week is the story.

Dell also described your problem better than anyone. "In most cases the model isn't the bottleneck to achieving enterprise scale for AI. The data is." Your knowledge is all there. It has never been written down in a form a machine can read.

None of this is built for a fifteen-person firm. That is not the point. The point is that the firms with the most to sell agree, in public and in one week, that a business's own knowledge was the missing part.

Your team already pays for the gap in hours

Glean's Work AI Index 2026, published 10 June 2026, found workers save about 11 hours a week through AI. It also found they spend 6.4 hours a week on what it calls botsitting: feeding in missing context, checking output, redoing prompts. The largest single slice is 2.3 hours a week loading context the tool should already have had.

Do the subtraction. Eleven hours saved, 6.4 spent making it usable, leaves 4.6. That is 42% of the advertised gain, and the productivity is being paid for twice. That 2.3 hours is three working weeks a year per person, spent producing nothing. For ten people at £25 an hour it is £29,900. All the botsitting is £83,200.

The same report explains why you feel busy rather than ahead. Only 13% of workers say their firm is performing significantly better. McKinsey's 2026 survey found the same at company level: 37% attribute at least some financial impact to AI, unchanged on the year before. And 53% of workers say the detail they need is not reachable through the AI systems they have.

Pasting everything in makes it worse

The shortcut everyone tries first is to dump every policy, deck and email thread into a tool that searches them. Chroma's research on context rot, published 14 July 2025, found that model performance is not consistent as input grows. More material can mean worse answers, not better ones.

There is a second, quieter failure. Someone says in a meeting that the delivery cut-off is Thursday. The note is saved. Three months later a system states, with total confidence, that the cut-off is Thursday. It was wrong when it was said, and now a machine has promoted it to company fact.

A pile of documents with no owner and no rule about which version wins gives you answers you cannot trace, correct or delete. That is worse than no memory at all, because it makes guesses look like facts.

What a company brain has to be able to do

Six things separate a brain from a chat window. It says "I don't know" instead of guessing. It shows which document, version and date an answer came from. It knows what was true when, so last year's price is not quoted as this year's. It respects permissions per item, because salary detail is not the holiday rota. A named person can correct it, and the correction sticks. It can forget one fact on request.

There is a seventh, and it is the commercial one. Your knowledge must survive a change of supplier. If what you know is welded to one vendor's assistant, changing vendor means rebuilding the most valuable thing you own.

Ask any supplier for all seven in writing before you buy, because none of it shows up in a demo.

What this means for you

Start by measuring, not by buying. Pick the three questions your team answers most often, and time how long each answer takes to find. That number, not a product demo, tells you whether this is worth doing. Ask any supplier to show you a source, a correction and a deletion before you sign anything.

What this means for you

Your business already knows the answer. The problem is that it lives in heads, inboxes and last month's meeting notes, so two staff give two answers. Company memory is a written, approved record with sources, versions and permissions. Fix that before you buy anything, and start by timing how long your team spends looking things up.

Questions people actually ask

What is a company brain in plain English?

It is a single, approved record of what your business knows, kept where your team and your software can both read it. Unlike a chatbot, it does not start from nothing every morning.

Can I just paste our documents into a chatbot?

You can, and the answers often get worse, because model performance drops as more material is added. Files with no owner and no rule about which version wins produce confident answers you cannot check.

How much does not having one cost?

Glean measured 2.3 hours a week per person spent feeding context a system should already have. For ten people at £25 an hour that is £29,900 a year, as an order of magnitude rather than an audit.
